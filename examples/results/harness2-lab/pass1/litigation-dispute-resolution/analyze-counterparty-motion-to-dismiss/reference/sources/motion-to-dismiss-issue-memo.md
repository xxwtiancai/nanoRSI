## II. RULE 12(b)(1) — SUBJECT MATTER JURISDICTION (WEAKEST PART OF THEIR MOTION)

**DataCore's argument:** The $1.2M consequential damages figure and the Harmon Ridge Capital lending consequences are "speculative and inflated," so the $75,000 amount in controversy is not met.

**Response:**
1. **Facial challenge standard.** DataCore raises a facial (not factual) attack — it cites only the Complaint, no affidavits on this point. On a facial challenge, the Court accepts the Complaint's well-pleaded allegations as true. Jurisdiction is established if the complaint claims damages in good faith above $75,000.
2. **Redundancy of the figures.** Even excluding the $1.2M consequential damages and punitive damages entirely, Pinnacle pleads $2,335,000 actual damages plus $931,000 reliance damages — facially sufficient. Dismissal requires appearance to a "legal certainty" that the threshold is unmet, impossible on these pleadings.
3. **Amount in controversy ≠ merits of damages.** Speculation as to the ultimate recoverable amount goes to proof, not jurisdiction. A short section suffices; do not over-invest here.

**Risk:** Low.

## III. RULE 12(b)(2) — PERSONAL JURISDICTION

**DataCore's argument:** All operations centralized in Austin; servers in Virginia/Oregon; Whitford Decl. ¶¶ 4–7, 13–14 (1,400 customers nationwide; nothing unique about Pinnacle; implementation delivered remotely). Relies on *Daimler* against general jurisdiction.

**Response — lead with specific jurisdiction:**
1. **Purposefully directed conduct in Georgia.** The Complaint alleges (¶¶ 12, 16, 25–44): Sousa, VP of Sales–Southeast, based at DataCore's Atlanta office at 100 Techwood Drive NW with ~14 employees; the misrepresentations (Jan. 10, 2022 email; Feb. 14, 2022 SOW Capabilities Document; brochure delivered in person in Atlanta; Jan. 18 and Feb. 7, 2022 demos) emanated from Georgia to a Georgia plaintiff; Sousa signed the MSA from Atlanta. Under *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462 (1985), and Georgia's long-arm statute (O.C.G.A. § 9-10-91), intentionally directing misrepresentations at a Georgia resident via Georgia-based personnel satisfies both the tort prong of the long-arm statute and minimum contacts.
2. **Claims arise out of the forum contacts.** The fraudulent-inducement and misrepresentation claims arise directly from Sousa's Georgia conduct — this is not a stream-of-commerce theory.
3. **Evidentiary/procedural point.** DataCore's declaration is from outside defense *counsel* (Whitford), largely on "information provided" by DataCore personnel (¶ 3) — hearsay-laden and of limited evidentiary weight. Critically, it nowhere denies the Atlanta office, the 14 employees, or Sousa's location; it describes Austin centralization only. Where the declaration does not controvert the complaint's jurisdictional allegations, Pinnacle's prima facie showing stands. Request jurisdictional discovery in the alternative.
4. **General jurisdiction (Compl. ¶ 17) is a loser under *Daimler*/*Goodyear*** — a 14-person regional office does not make DataCore "essentially at home" in Georgia. Present it only as an alternative, or drop it and argue specific jurisdiction exclusively.

**Risk:** Low for specific jurisdiction. Highlight DataCore's conspicuous failure to deny the Atlanta office facts.

## IV. RULE 12(b)(3) / FORUM SELECTION CLAUSE — OUR TOUGHEST FIGHT

**DataCore's argument:** MSA § 14.2 mandates exclusive resolution in Travis County, Texas courts; the clause is mandatory, valid, and covers all claims; Pinnacle does not allege fraud specific to the clause itself.

**Responses:**
1. **Correct legal vehicle — DataCore's biggest doctrinal error.** Under *Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court*, 571 U.S. 49 (2013), a forum selection clause does **not** render venue "improper" under 28 U.S.C. § 1391; Rule 12(b)(3) dismissal is therefore the wrong vehicle. The proper mechanism is a § 1404(a) transfer motion (where venue is otherwise proper). Venue is proper in N.D. Ga. under § 1391(b)(2) because substantial events giving rise to the claims occurred in-district — the misrepresentations were made here by Georgia-based personnel. *Atlantic Marine* is conspicuously absent from DataCore's authority table (they rely on the pre-*Atlantic Marine* *Bremen/Stewart/Scherk* framework). This is a clean, decisive lead argument for denying Rule 12(b)(3) relief.
2. **Fraud claims may fall outside or vitiate the clause.** Counts II–III challenge pre-contractual conduct. Argue: (a) where a party is fraudulently induced to enter a contract containing a forum clause, the clause may be attacked as a product of the same fraud (the allegations of a rigged staging-environment demo support a scheme reaching the contract as a whole); and (b) the clause covers disputes "arising out of or relating to this Agreement" — tort claims premised on pre-contractual representations that were never incorporated into the MSA (Compl. ¶ 39: the SOW Capabilities Document was a standalone document) arguably fall outside the clause's scope.
3. **Public-interest factors (for any § 1404(a) motion).** Under *Atlantic Marine*, private-interest factors and plaintiff's convenience are discounted, but public-interest factors remain: Georgia's strong local interest in policing misrepresentations directed at a Georgia business from a Georgia office; Georgia-based witnesses (Nance, Lofton, Holliday, Harmon Ridge, Greenleaf); and the Georgia UDTPA claim. Be candid with the client that transfer risk is nonetheless real.
4. **Practical fallback:** If the Court honors the clause, seek § 1404(a) transfer (not dismissal) to W.D. Tex. (Austin Division, which includes Travis County), preserving the claims and avoiding re-filing/limitations exposure — especially important given DataCore's § 9.4 one-year-limitations theory.

**Risk:** Moderate–high that the case ultimately lands in Texas, but Rule 12(b)(3) *dismissal* itself should fail.

## V. RULE 12(b)(6) — COUNT I (BREACH OF CONTRACT)

### A. Contractual one-year limitations period (MSA § 9.4)

**DataCore's argument:** Pinnacle knew of the breaches by August 2022 (ADP concerns) and no later than the December 5, 2022 formal notice letter; suit filed March 22, 2024 — more than one year later.

**Responses:**
1. **Accrual is a fact question.** Dismissal on limitations grounds is appropriate only if untimeliness appears on the face of the pleadings. The § 9.4 trigger — when Pinnacle "knew or should have known of the facts giving rise to such claim" — cannot be resolved on the pleadings, particularly where DataCore repeatedly promised and attempted remediation.
2. **Estoppel/tolling via promised repairs.** The December 5, 2022 notice letter, the January 12, 2023 Subramanian email (acknowledging limitations and outlining a patch plan), and the April 15, 2023 patch support equitable estoppel or tolling: DataCore's assurances of cure reasonably induced Pinnacle to defer suit while performance remained uncertain.
3. **Continuing breach.** Monthly non-conforming performance (availability targets under § 2.2/Exhibit C SLA; integration functionality under Article 4) constitutes a continuing breach; each month's failure restarts the clock, at minimum for damages within the year preceding filing (covering the failed-patch period from roughly March 2023 forward).
4. **Reasonableness as applied (backup).** A one-year clock arguably beginning *before* implementation was complete and accepted (June 30, 2022, with the § 3.3 acceptance process) is unreasonably short as applied; preserve for development.

### B. Limitation of liability (§ 11.1) and consequential damages waiver (§ 11.2)

**DataCore's argument:** Liability capped at $432,000 (12 months of fees); consequential and punitive damages mutually waived.

**Responses:**
1. **A damages cap does not support dismissal of a claim.** At most §§ 11.1–11.2 limit remedies; Count I states a claim regardless. These provisions cannot properly be used under 12(b)(6) to kill the count itself.
2. **"Event giving rise to the claim" ambiguity.** § 11.1 caps liability at fees paid in the 12 months preceding "the event giving rise to the claim" — if the operative events include the failed April 2023 patch or later performance failures, the cap's calculation is a fact question.
3. **Willful/fraudulent conduct carve-outs and public policy.** Under both Texas and Georgia law, limitation-of-liability and damages waivers do not shield intentional or fraudulent conduct; enforcement against fraud claims (and fraud-based punitive damages) violates public policy. §§ 11.1–11.2 therefore do not reach Counts II–III or punitive damages.
4. **Candid assessment:** the consequential-damages waiver is our weakest damages issue as to *contract* damages ($1.2M replacement costs/lost business). Preserve the argument that replacement-system costs are *direct/general* damages (benefit of the bargain / cost of cover), not consequential.

## VI. RULE 12(b)(6) — COUNT II (FRAUDULENT INDUCEMENT)

**DataCore's arguments:** (a) insufficient particularity under Rule 9(b)/*Twombly–Iqbal*; (b) integration clause (§ 14.7) precludes reliance; (c) the representations were non-binding pre-sale "puffery."

**Responses:**
1. **Particularity is satisfied.** The Complaint pleads the who (Sousa; Baines), what (ADP "out of the box with zero custom configuration"; "15,000 concurrent temporary worker records with sub-second latency"; brochure's "native integration with 40+ payroll and HRIS platforms"), when (Jan. 10, 2022 email; Jan. 18 and Feb. 7, 2022 demos; Feb. 14, 2022 SOW Capabilities Document), where (from DataCore's Atlanta office to Pinnacle in Atlanta), and reliance and causation (MSA execution; $931,000 paid; ~$85,000 custom API development; 4,000-record actual capacity — a ~73% shortfall; 7,500 even after the patch).
2. **Scienter supported by DataCore's own CTO.** The January 12, 2023 Subramanian email concedes "performance limitations at scale that do not meet the capabilities described during the sales process." A contemporaneous admission from DataCore's CTO supports knowledge and defeats the puffery characterization — these were specific, measurable, falsifiable technical claims, not opinion or sales talk.
3. **The integration clause is not a bar at the pleading stage.** Texas and Georgia require a clear, specific, and unambiguous *disclaimer-of-reliance* to defeat justifiable reliance; § 14.7 is a standard merger clause with no such language. Moreover, fraudulent inducement attacks the contract's validity; a defendant cannot use the product of the alleged fraud (the integration clause) to immunize itself — the argument is circular. And because the SOW Capabilities Document was never attached to or incorporated in the MSA (Compl. ¶ 39), the misrepresentations come from outside the integrated writing.
4. **Anticipate the sophistication attack** (Pinnacle, $48M revenue, counseled). Counter: the representations were highly technical capacity claims that Pinnacle's CTO could not independently verify pre-sale, and DataCore controlled the demo environment (allegedly rigged staging environment, Compl. ¶ 34).

**Risk:** Low–moderate.

## VII. RULE 12(b)(6) — COUNT III (NEGLIGENT MISREPRESENTATION)

**DataCore's arguments:** integration clause; economic loss rule precludes tort recovery for purely economic loss.

**Responses:**
1. **Independent-duty exception.** Both Texas (negligent misrepresentation by one supplying false information for another's guidance in a business transaction) and Georgia (misrepresentation by one with superior special knowledge, *Robert & Co. v. Rhodes-Haverty* line) permit tort recovery where the duty arises independent of the contract. A vendor making pre-contractual technical capability representations to induce a purchase owes an independent duty to speak accurately.
2. **Otherwise mirror the Count II responses** on particularity, reliance, and the merger clause.

**Risk:** Moderate.

## VIII. RULE 12(b)(6) — COUNT IV (GEORGIA UDTPA, O.C.G.A. § 10-1-370 ET SEQ.)

**DataCore's arguments:** duplicative of contract; economic loss rule; Texas choice of law (§ 14.3) precludes a Georgia statutory claim.

**Responses:**
1. **Choice of law is premature.** Section 14.3 governs disputes "arising under this Agreement"; whether the clause reaches a statutory claim premised on pre-contractual tortious conduct occurring in Georgia is a disputed clause-scope question unsuitable for resolution on a 12(b)(6) record. Georgia also has a strong public interest in applying its deceptive-trade-practices law to conduct directed at Georgia businesses from within Georgia.
2. **Candid assessment — do not overinvest.** The GDTPA's orientation toward injunctive relief makes this our most vulnerable count (post-termination, Pinnacle's injunctive-relief standing is questionable). Count IV is a candidate for a bargaining-chip concession in briefing or negotiation, while preserving the underlying misconduct allegations through Counts II–III.
