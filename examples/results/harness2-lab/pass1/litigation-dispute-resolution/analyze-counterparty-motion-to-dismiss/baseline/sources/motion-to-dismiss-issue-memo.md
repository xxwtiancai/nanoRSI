## I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

DataCore's four-pronged motion is vulnerable on nearly every ground. The strongest points for our opposition:

1. **Rule 12(b)(2) (personal jurisdiction) is DataCore's weakest argument.** The Complaint alleges DataCore maintained a permanent Atlanta office staffed by ~14 employees, and that Victor Sousa — DataCore's VP of Sales, Southeast Region — made the alleged misrepresentations *from Georgia* and signed the MSA from the Atlanta office. These allegations establish purposeful availment and claims arising out of forum contacts under *Burger King*. Notably, the Whitford Declaration **conspicuously never denies the existence of the Atlanta office or Sousa's Georgia base** — a critical gap.
2. **Rule 12(b)(1) is meritless.** Diversity is complete on the face of the Complaint (Georgia vs. Delaware/Texas), and the amount in controversy plainly exceeds $75,000 on its face; a defendant cannot defeat jurisdiction by relitigating the merits of damages figures.
3. **Rule 12(b)(3) is likely the wrong vehicle and wrong on the merits.** Under *Atlantic Marine* (not cited by DataCore), a forum-selection clause pointing to another federal forum is enforced through § 1404(a) transfer or forum non conveniens — **not** Rule 12(b)(3). And even on the merits, enforcing the clause would contravene Georgia public policy behind the Georgia UDTPA.
4. **Rule 12(b)(6) — Count I (contract)**: The one-year limitations period in MSA § 9.4 is DataCore's strongest single argument. Our best counters are (a) the January 12, 2023 Subramanian email acknowledging "performance limitations … that do not meet the capabilities described during the sales process" arguably re-started or extended accrual/estops DataCore from invoking § 9.4; (b) § 9.4 by its terms applies to claims "arising under" the MSA and should not time-bar the tort claims; and (c) the accrual date is a fact question ill-suited to Rule 12.
5. **Rule 12(b)(6) — Counts II–IV (fraud, negligent misrepresentation, UDTPA)**: The Complaint pleads the *who, what, when, where, and how* of the fraud with particularity (Sousa's January 10, 2022 email; the February 14, 2022 SOW Capabilities Document; the staged demos; and the Subramanian admission). The integration clause (§ 14.7) does not bar fraudulent-inducement claims as a matter of law. The economic loss rule does not bar fraud claims involving independent duties / misrepresentations that induce entry into the contract, and does not apply to the UDTPA claim.

**Overall assessment:** We should have strong prospects of defeating the motion in substantial part. The venue/§ 1404(a) transfer risk is the most realistic adverse outcome short of dismissal; the § 9.4 limitations argument is the most realistic partial-dismissal risk (as to Count I only).

## III. RULE 12(b)(1) — SUBJECT MATTER JURISDICTION

**DataCore's argument:** Damages figures ($1.2M consequential; $931K reliance; $2.335M actual) are "speculative and inflated"; amount in controversy therefore not established.

**Analysis / Opposition points:**

- Complete diversity is facially alleged and undisputed: Pinnacle is a Georgia citizen; DataCore is a Delaware corporation with its principal place of business in Austin, Texas (Complaint ¶¶ 7–10; Whitford Decl. ¶¶ 4–5). DataCore does not contest diversity.
- At the pleading stage, the amount in controversy is tested by the **face of the complaint**, and dismissal is proper only to a "legal certainty" that the claim is below $75,000. *St. Paul Mercury Indem. Co. v. Red Cab Co.*, 303 U.S. 283 (1938). The Complaint seeks in excess of $3.5M plus punitives. Legal-certainty dismissal is untenable.
- Even the facially "speculative" categories survive: the $931,000 in fees actually paid is a documented, non-speculative figure; the $1.2M consequential figure is attributed to Pinnacle's auditor (Greenleaf Accounting Group). Damages quantum is a merits question, not a jurisdictional one.
- DataCore's own motion concedes Pinnacle "asserts total damages of approximately $4,466,000" — it cannot simultaneously claim legal certainty below $75,000.

**Bottom line:** 12(b)(1) dismissal should be easily defeated.

## IV. RULE 12(b)(2) — PERSONAL JURISDICTION (KEY BATTLEGROUND)

**DataCore's argument:** Relies on *Daimler*/*Goodyear* — DataCore is "at home" in Texas/Austin; Atlanta office is a mere regional office; interactions were remote (cloud platform hosted in Virginia/Oregon; implementation delivered remotely per Whitford Decl. ¶ 14).

**Analysis / Opposition points:**

- **Specific jurisdiction — purposeful availment.** The Complaint alleges (¶¶ 12, 16, 25, 44): (a) a permanent Atlanta office at 100 Techwood Drive with ~14 employees; (b) Sousa, VP of Sales for the Southeast, based in Atlanta; (c) Sousa made the alleged misrepresentations from Georgia; (d) Sousa signed the MSA from the Atlanta office; (e) the sales process targeted Pinnacle in Georgia. Under *Burger King v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462 (1985), deliberately exploiting the Georgia market through Georgia-based personnel is purposeful availment.
- **Claims arise out of forum contacts.** The tort claims arise directly from Sousa's Georgia-directed representations — a direct causal nexus. The contract claim arises from an agreement solicited and executed by Georgia-based personnel. *Daimler* and *Goodyear* address **general** jurisdiction ("at home") and do not cabin specific jurisdiction; DataCore's reliance on them misses the mark.
- **General jurisdiction (alternative pleading).** This is our weaker theory under *Daimler* (an Atlanta regional office with 14 employees will not make DataCore "essentially at home" in Georgia vs. Austin headquarters). We should lead with specific jurisdiction and treat general jurisdiction as a fallback only.
- **Critical evidentiary gap in the Whitford Declaration.** The Declaration details Austin operations, Texas incorporation, Delaware incorporation, cloud hosting, and remote implementation — but **nowhere denies** that DataCore maintains the Atlanta office, that ~14 employees work there, or that Sousa is/was based there. On a 12(b)(2) motion, uncontroverted allegations in the complaint (especially where the supporting declaration is silent) must be taken as true, and we satisfy our prima facie burden. This silence should be highlighted prominently.
- **Waiver/consent point:** Section 14.2 consents to jurisdiction in Travis County, Texas but does not purport to *waive* jurisdiction elsewhere as to pre-contract torts; and it is not a defense to personal jurisdiction in Georgia under the long-arm statute anyway.

**Bottom line:** Strong likelihood of defeating 12(b)(2). Recommend an accompanying Nance declaration (or Sousa emails showing his Atlanta signature block — the January 10, 2022 email itself bears "100 Techwood Drive NW, Atlanta, GA 30318" in the signature, Complaint ¶ 28) to fortify the prima facie showing.

## V. RULE 12(b)(3) — VENUE / FORUM SELECTION CLAUSE

**DataCore's argument:** MSA § 14.2 mandates exclusive resolution in Travis County, Texas courts; venue in N.D. Ga. is improper.

**Analysis / Opposition points:**

1. **Wrong procedural vehicle.** Under *Atlantic Marine Constr. Co. v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 571 U.S. 49 (2013), a forum-selection clause does not render venue "improper" under 28 U.S.C. § 1391/Rule 12(b)(3). Venue is proper in N.D. Ga. under § 1391(b)(2) — substantial events (the alleged misrepresentations, negotiation, execution by Georgia-based personnel, injury) occurred in this District (Complaint ¶ 18). The exclusive remedy is a § 1404(a) transfer motion (or forum non conveniens for a state-court clause), which shifts to DataCore the burden of showing the clause covers all claims and that transfer serves the parties' and public interest — with the plaintiff's choice given weight *only* via the clause. DataCore's motion cites *Bremen*/*Stewart Org.* but not *Atlantic Marine*; the omission is telling and should be exploited.
2. **Scope of the clause.** § 14.2 covers disputes "arising out of or relating to this Agreement." The fraudulent-inducement, negligent-misrepresentation, and UDTPA claims rest on **pre-contractual** conduct (Dec. 2021–Feb. 2022 emails, demos, brochure, SOW Capabilities Document). While "relating to" is broad, we should argue the tort claims fall outside (or at minimum present a fact dispute) because the SOW Capabilities Document was expressly *not* incorporated into the MSA (Complaint ¶ 39).
3. **Public policy.** Even under *Atlantic Marine*, a transfer that would contravene a strong public policy of the transferor forum may be denied. Georgia's UDTPA embodies such a policy; enforcing Texas law (§ 14.3, "arising under this Agreement") to displace the Georgia statutory claim is a further reason to resist transfer.
4. **Consequences of transfer are not dismissal.** At worst, DataCore is entitled to consider a § 1404(a) transfer — not dismissal of the action. The motion's requested relief (dismissal under 12(b)(3)) is categorically unavailable under *Atlantic Marine*.

**Bottom line:** 12(b)(3) dismissal should be defeated; the realistic risk is a § 1404(a) transfer to W.D. Tex. (Austin Division). Even there, the tort-claims-scope and public-policy arguments give us a meaningful fight.

## VI. RULE 12(b)(6) — FAILURE TO STATE A CLAIM

### A. Count I — Breach of Contract

**DataCore's arguments:** (i) time-barred by MSA § 9.4 (one year from knew/should-have-known); (ii) capped by § 11.1 liability cap ($432K); (iii) barred by § 11.2 consequential-damages waiver.

**Opposition points:**

- **Limitations — accrual is a fact question.** § 9.4 runs from when Pinnacle "knew or should have known of the facts giving rise to such claim." Even accepting August 2022 as discovery of the ADP issue, the Complaint alleges a continuing course of conduct: the latency problem surfaced in November 2022; formal notice December 5, 2022; and DataCore's CTO Subramanian acknowledged on **January 12, 2023** "performance limitations at scale that do not meet the capabilities described during the sales process" and promised a patch — delivered April 15, 2023 and still deficient (7,500 vs. 15,000 records). Suit filed March 22, 2024. We should argue: (a) DataCore's January 2023 acknowledgment and remediation promise **estops** or tolls the running of § 9.4 (equitable estoppel: a party cannot lull a counterparty with promised fixes and then plead the limitations clause); (b) breaches continued (each month of non-conforming performance is a fresh breach); and (c) the accrual date cannot be resolved on the pleadings. Note: March 22, 2024 is within one year of the April 15, 2023 patch and within one year of continued use/payment through December 2023.
- **Scope of § 9.4.** By its terms § 9.4 governs claims "arising under" the MSA; the tort and statutory claims should not be time-barred by it in any event.
- **Liability cap and damages waiver are affirmative defenses, not pleading bars.** Sections 11.1/11.2 do not eliminate the claim; at most they limit remedies. Their applicability (including whether they are enforceable, unconscionable, or inapplicable to intentional/wanton conduct) is not resolvable on a Rule 12 motion. Additionally, if DataCore retains the Texas choice-of-law clause (§ 14.3), Texas Bus. & Com. Code § 2.725 (cited by DataCore itself) permits a 4-year limitations period and allows contractual reduction only to not less than one year — neutral at best for DataCore on timeliness for the contract claim (one-year contractual period is enforceable under Texas law, so accrual/estoppel arguments carry the load).
- **Well-pleaded breach:** MSA § 2.4(a) obligates Provider to deliver the Platform "in material conformity with the Documentation"; § 4.1 addresses standard integrations. The alleged failures (no out-of-box ADP integration; 4,000-record capacity vs. represented capability) plausibly state non-conforming performance.

### B. Count II — Fraudulent Inducement

**DataCore's arguments:** Not pleaded with particularity (Rule 9(b)); integration clause (§ 14.7) precludes reliance; mere "generalized dissatisfaction."

**Opposition points:**

- **Rule 9(b) is satisfied.** The Complaint pleads with specificity: *who* (Sousa; Baines), *what* ("fully integrates … out of the box with zero custom configuration"; "up to 15,000 concurrent temporary worker records with sub-second latency"; "native integration with 40+ payroll and HRIS platforms"), *when* (Jan. 10, 2022 email; Jan. 18 & Feb. 7, 2022 demos; Nov. 2021 brochure; Feb. 14, 2022 SOW Capabilities Document), *where* (from DataCore's Atlanta office, per email signature blocks), *how relied upon* (execution of the $2,335,000 MSA; rejection of competing vendors), and *falsity/knowledge* (actual capacity ~4,000 records; ~$85,000 custom API work required; staged demo environment; and DataCore CTO Subramanian's written admission on Jan. 12, 2023 that the platform had "performance limitations at scale that do not meet the capabilities described during the sales process").
- **Integration clause is not a bar at the pleading stage.** Under both Georgia and Texas law, fraudulent inducement is not negated by an integration clause unless the clause contains an express disclaimer of reliance on the specific representations at issue (e.g., *Italian Colors* line of authority; Georgia: *Woesten v. Advance Die Co.*; Texas: *Forest Oil/Schlumberger* factors). Section 14.7 is a generic merger clause with **no express non-reliance language**, so the bar does not apply as a matter of law, particularly given the disparity in knowledge about technical capabilities.
- **Justifiable reliance is a jury question**, especially for representations of technical fact (concurrency capacity, native integration) that a buyer could not independently verify pre-purchase.

### C. Count III — Negligent Misrepresentation

- **Economic loss rule.** DataCore argues the claim seeks purely economic loss from a contractual relationship. Counter: the alleged misrepresentations were **pre-contractual** and **induced** the contract; both Georgia and Texas law allow negligent-misrepresentation claims for misinformation supplied to guide the plaintiff's business decision where the duty arises independently of the contract. Discovery of the staged demo environment also supports an independent-duty theory.
- **Integration clause:** same analysis as Count II — no express non-reliance disclaimer.
- Concede this is our most vulnerable tort count; the primary defense is the independent-duty/inducement framing.

### D. Count IV — Georgia UDTPA (O.C.G.A. § 10-1-370 et seq.)

**DataCore's arguments:** duplicative of contract; economic loss rule; Texas choice of law displaces it.

**Opposition points:**

- The UDTPA targets *prospective* trade practices (deceptive acts in connection with sale/purchase of goods/services directed at consumers or prospective purchasers). The alleged deception occurred during the **pre-sale process** — squarely within the statutory zone.
- Choice of law: § 14.3 governs disputes "arising under this Agreement"; the UDTPA claim rests on pre-contractual conduct, and Georgia has a strong public policy interest in policing deceptive sales practices aimed at Georgia businesses. Whether the clause reaches this claim is a choice-of-law question unsuitable for Rule 12 resolution.
- Duplicativeness: the fraud-based statutory claim is not "duplicative" for pleading purposes; in any event dismissal is not warranted where the claim rests on distinct deceptive conduct (staged demos, brochure).

### E. Cross-Cutting: Choice of Law

- DataCore's insistence on Texas law cuts both ways: Texas law also recognizes fraudulent inducement notwithstanding generic merger clauses where no express non-reliance disclaimer exists, and § 2.725's savings features limit contractual shortening of limitations to no less than one year (met here, but accrual is disputed). We should argue that regardless of Georgia or Texas law, the Complaint survives.
