## I. Executive Summary

Plaintiff moves for partial summary judgment on the federal WARN Act claim, arguing (1) each facility independently triggered WARN coverage, (2) alternatively all three facilities are a single site of employment, (3) Greenleaf concededly gave zero notice, and (4) neither the unforeseeable business circumstances (UBC) exception nor the faltering company exception applies. The zero-notice fact is effectively admitted (Answer ¶ 23), so the opposition cannot contest liability outright. The viable strategy is to defeat summary judgment by establishing genuine disputes of material fact on (a) whether Huntsville independently triggers coverage, (b) the single-site issue, (c) foreseeability of the SunHarvest non-renewal, and (d) application of the UBC and faltering company exceptions — and to limit damages even if liability is found. There are also identifiable weaknesses and errors in Plaintiff's own papers (Part IV below) that should be exploited.

---

## II. Adverse Facts We Must Confront

These facts are admitted or poorly contested and frame the risk profile:

1. **Zero notice is admitted.** Greenleaf's Answer (¶ 23) admits no 60-day notice was given to employees, Tennessee, Alabama, or any local government. Liability for *some* violation as to Nashville and Chattanooga is very difficult to avoid; the fight is over scope, exceptions, and damages.
2. **Timeline.** 87 days elapsed between the October 22, 2024 SunHarvest non-renewal letter and the January 17, 2025 Nashville/Chattanooga terminations; 94 days to Huntsville (SUMF ¶ 16; Headcount Data).
3. **CEO's September 2024 suspicion.** Voss testified she "suspected as early as September 2024 that SunHarvest might not renew" (Voss Dep. 45:14–16; 15:3–5). Plaintiff uses this to argue foreseeability.
4. **Kowalski's warnings.** VP-HR Kowalski raised WARN compliance in mid-November 2024, was told to "hold off on notices pending the outcome of business development efforts," prepared a draft notice template around November 25–26, 2024, and admitted on redirect that notices sent when she first raised the issue (~Nov. 15) would have achieved mathematical compliance (63 days before Jan. 17) (Kowalski Dep. 47–52, 163).
5. **Huntsville employees received no notice even for the final week** after the January 17 Nashville/Chattanooga terminations, deliberately, to protect contract fulfillment (Voss Dep. 38–40). Bad optics; must be handled carefully.
6. **No severance or pay in lieu of notice** was paid (SUMF ¶ 32), eliminating any § 2104 offset argument and maximizing exposure (~$4.88M claimed).
7. **Financial deterioration:** $8.2M cash vs. $14.6M monthly operating costs as of January 1, 2025 (Chen Dep.).

## III. Plaintiff's Motion — Weaknesses and Opposition Strategies

### A. Coverage / Threshold Arguments

**1. Huntsville does not independently trigger WARN coverage — Plaintiff's argument here is legally and factually wrong.**
- A "mass layoff" under 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(3) requires at least 50 employees terminated at a *single site* (and 33%), or 500+ employees. Huntsville: 37 of 143 (25.9%) — fails **both** the 50-employee and 33% thresholds.
- Plaintiff's own Headcount Data (Ex. F, Plant Summary) asserts "Meets 50+ Terminated Threshold: Yes (37 ≥ 50)" — a facially false statement. The motion's Section I.B asserts Huntsville "independently satisfies" coverage only through the alternative single-site theory, and the SUMF never claims Huntsville independently triggers.
- **Strategy:** Expressly concede-and-defeat: move to strike or correct the erroneous Headcount characterization; argue Huntsville liability exists *only* if the single-site theory succeeds. This is our strongest coverage point.

**2. Single site of employment — contestable.**
- The three plants are in different cities and two states, are not in reasonable geographic proximity, do not share staff or equipment, and no employees commute between them — each factor under 20 C.F.R. § 639.3(i)(2) favors Greenleaf. Answer ¶ 30 correctly pleads this.
- Plaintiff relies on centralized management/HR/payroll (Kowalski Dep. 12–13) and the unified Pinnacle plan — but DOL regulations treat centralized administration as insufficient where facilities are geographically separate; the "single site" test is location-focused. Teamsters Local 413 and Rifkin involved far more integration.
- **Strategy:** Develop facts on separate plant managers, separate supervisory structures, distinct operational functions, no shared commuting/equipment (already pleaded in Answer ¶ 30). This defeats Huntsville coverage entirely and, if successful, eliminates ~37 employees from the class ($ range roughly $500K+ in claimed damages).

**3. Nashville and Chattanooga independently trigger coverage — concede.**
- 138/312 (44.2%) and 72/189 (38.1%) plainly meet § 2101(a)(3). Do not waste credibility contesting this. Note Voss confirmed no plant closed; all three remain operating (Voss Dep. 32), which actually helps against any "plant closing" characterization but does not defeat mass layoff.

### B. The Exceptions (the Core of the Opposition)

**4. Unforeseeable Business Circumstances Exception, 29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(2)(A) — genuine factual dispute exists.**
- Elements: the layoff was caused by business circumstances not reasonably foreseeable at the time notice would have been required, and the employer gave as much notice as practicable.
- **Foreseeability — favorable facts:**
  - SunHarvest had renewed annually since 2014; it made similar "exploring alternatives" noises in 2021 and 2022 and renewed both times (Voss Dep. 15:18–16:5). The October 22 letter was the *first written* indication of non-renewal ("That's when I knew it was real," Voss Dep. 16:23).
  - Voss personally lobbied SunHarvest's CEO, offered pricing concessions, and explored a scaled-down arrangement through early November — the outcome remained uncertain (Voss Dep. 17:10–16).
  - The *scope* of layoffs was unknown until Pinnacle's December 20 report; Kowalski confirmed on cross that in mid-November no one knew whether 50+ employees at any single plant would be affected (Kowalski Dep. 148, 150). Under DOL guidance (20 C.F.R. § 639.9), unforeseeability is measured against the *time notice would have been required*, and the identity/scope of the layoff matters.
  - The *immediate trigger* of the full-scale RIF was the sequential collapse of the Ridgewater credit line (Jan. 8) and the FreshPath LOI (Jan. 10) — days before the January 17 terminations (Voss Dep. 29–31; Kowalski Dep. 155).
- **Adverse facts to manage:** Voss's September 2024 "suspicion"; the 87-day gap; Kowalski's mid-November warning that mid-November notices were needed. Frame foreseeability of the *contract loss* as distinct from foreseeability of a *mass layoff of this scope at each site*; emphasize that a customer's sudden termination of 31% of revenue ($57.97M) is a paradigmatic UBC.
- **"As much notice as practicable" — the weak prong.** Zero notice and the "hold off" direction hurt. Counter with: Kowalski's draft notice template (Nov. 25–26) shows preparation; the situation remained "very fluid" (Kowalski Dep. 150); the company was actively pursuing FreshPath, Ridgewater, Harmon, and Blue Ridge to avoid layoffs entirely; and financial exigency made 60 more days of full payroll impossible (cash < 1 month of operating costs). Note: the Kowalski mid-November "hold off" testimony may be vulnerable to an attorney-client privilege challenge (defense counsel's instruction not to answer was partially overruled by the witness answering anyway); consider a motion to strike those excerpts or argue they reveal privileged communications.

**5. Faltering Company Exception, 29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(1) — genuinely disputed.**
- Elements: (i) company actively seeking capital or business that would have avoided or postponed the layoff; (ii) company had a realistic opportunity to obtain it; (iii) employer reasonably and in good faith believed the 60-day notice would have precluded the capital/business.
- **Favorable facts:** Ridgewater $15M credit application (Dec. 12) and FreshPath $22M LOI (Dec. 5) were concrete, documented pursuits. Voss testified she believed sending layoff notices would have killed the FreshPath deal and caused loss of key personnel ("we would have lost good people for nothing," Voss Dep. 31:8–12); Kowalski confirmed FreshPath might have cut layoffs to 100–120 positions (Kowalski Dep. 68). An LOI and a formal credit application are more than "preliminary discussions."
- **Adverse facts:** FreshPath was non-binding and replaced only ~38% of lost revenue (Chen Dep. 58:2–14; SUMF ¶ 21); Kowalski told Voss on December 19 that even with FreshPath "we'd still need to do significant layoffs" (Kowalski Dep. 93); Harmon was "a couple of phone calls and one lunch" and Blue Ridge a single introductory meeting (Voss Dep. 29–30).
- **Strategy:** Emphasize the exception requires only a *realistic opportunity*, not a certainty; the LOI was signed and active until January 10. Kowalski's Dec. 19 statement creates a fact dispute on good faith — argue it shows management genuinely wrestling with scope, not bad faith. At minimum, these competing reasonable inferences defeat summary judgment under *Anderson*.

**6. Anticipatory / conditional notice argument.** Plaintiff argues (Motion § II.C) the exceptions never excuse "complete inaction." Counter: the cases require notice "as soon as practicable," which is a fact-bound, reasonableness inquiry ill-suited to summary judgment; the jury must weigh the timing decisions against the fluid pursuit of FreshPath/Ridgewater.

### C. Damages

**7. Attack the damages calculation.**
- Average daily rate $329.47 × 247 × 60 ≈ $4.88M (SUMF ¶ 31). Contest:
  - **Mitigation offsets:** § 2104(a)(2) requires deduction of interim earnings and certain benefits; damages must be calculated employee-by-employee (Briggs herself found new work in ~3 months). Individualized mitigation inquiries also support Rule 23 predominance arguments (Answer ¶ 56).
  - **Benefits component:** demand proof of actual medical/benefit costs, not estimates.
  - **Data integrity issues in Plaintiff's own Exhibit F** (see Part IV) — undermine reliability of the payroll figures.
  - **Class-scope damages:** if Huntsville is excluded (no independent trigger; no single site), damages drop by the 37 Huntsville employees.
  - **Civil penalties ($500/day per local government unit):** arguable only upon a "violation"; resist where exceptions apply and note penalty avoidable upon reasonable-good-faith showing under § 2104(a)(4) ("employer acted in good faith and reasonably believed" — tie to advice-of-counsel and Kowalski's consultations with Ms. Ashford).

## IV. Errors and Weaknesses in Plaintiff's Papers (Exploit These)

1. **Headcount Data error:** "Meets 50+ Terminated Threshold: Yes (37 ≥ 50)" for Huntsville — mathematically false on the face of Plaintiff's own exhibit. Move to strike or highlight in opposition.
2. **Motion overstatement:** The motion (§ I.B) implies Huntsville independently triggers coverage; the SUMF never says so, and the correct statute refutes it.
3. **Suspicious payroll entries:** "Chen, David — CFO" appears in the Nashville termination detail (GRN-1005) despite Chen being CFO and a deposition witness; duplicate/conflicting names (e.g., Angela Briggs vs. Angela Green; multiple Sanders/Reed employees) warrant a records audit — data reliability challenge to the damages exhibit.
4. **SUMF ¶ 32 & Kowalski excerpts:** portions derive from arguably privileged communications with outside counsel (Kowalski Dep. 47–49 objections). Renew privilege objection; alternatively seek to strike the "hold off" testimony.
5. **Briggs Declaration ¶ 12** states a legal conclusion ("The company violated the WARN Act") — move to strike as improper legal opinion; ¶¶ 10–11 (hardship) are irrelevant to WARN liability (which is limited to back pay/benefits, not consequential damages like mortgage arrears and credit card debt) — move to strike as irrelevant and prejudicial.
6. **Briggs ¶ 7** (supervisor said "everything is fine") — potentially a detrimental reliance/fraud angle, but also confirms *rumors* of the SunHarvest loss circulated in November, which cuts both ways; handle with care.
7. **Reddick bar numbers differ** between the motion (TN Bar No. 034871) and SUMF (034782) and addresses differ (195 vs. 191 Peachtree) — minor, but note for accuracy challenges.

## V. Procedural / Strategic Recommendations

1. **File a timely opposition with a counter-statement of disputed material facts** under L.R. 56.01 responding paragraph-by-paragraph to the SUMF, admitting the zero-notice fact but disputing foreseeability, single-site, exception, and damages facts.
2. **Support with declarations** from Voss (prior-year renewal history; belief in FreshPath; advice of counsel), Chen (financial exigency; Ridgewater), Kowalski (fluidity of scope; draft notice preparation; FreshPath contingency), and plant managers (site separation facts).
3. **Move to strike or exclude:** (a) the erroneous Huntsville threshold line in Exhibit F; (b) privileged Kowalski testimony; (c) legal conclusions and irrelevant hardship testimony in the Briggs Declaration.
4. **Primary framing:** the dispositive questions — foreseeability, good faith, realistic opportunity to obtain capital, and "as much notice as practicable" — are quintessential jury questions turning on credibility and reasonableness; summary judgment is inappropriate under *Anderson* and *Matsushita*.
5. **Fallback positions:** if partial summary judgment is granted as to Nashville/Chattanooga liability, oppose entry on exceptions and damages; press individualized mitigation and Rule 23 predominance defenses; preserve good-faith penalty defense under § 2104(a)(4).
6. **Settlement leverage assessment:** conceded zero notice, admitted 87-day runway, and Kowalski's math concession make full defense of Nashville/Chattanooga liability risky; realistic exposure is ~$4.4M gross for those two plants before offsets. Evaluate early mediation focused on the exceptions' jury risk for both sides.

---

## VI. Conclusion

Summary judgment should be opposed on the grounds that (1) Huntsville does not independently trigger WARN and the single-site theory is factually disputed; (2) the UBC and faltering company exceptions present genuine disputes of material fact regarding foreseeability, good faith, and practicability; and (3) Plaintiff's damages figures rest on an exhibit containing facial errors and are subject to substantial individualized offsets. Recommend immediate work on supporting declarations, the privilege motion, and the strike requests identified above.
