## I. Executive Summary

Plaintiff moves for partial summary judgment on the federal WARN Act claim, resting on three pillars: (1) each of the three plants independently triggers WARN coverage (or, alternatively, all three constitute a single site of employment); (2) neither the unforeseeable business circumstances exception nor the faltering company exception applies; and (3) damages should be awarded at the statutory maximum of 60 days for all 247 terminated employees.

The motion is vulnerable on each pillar. Critically: **Huntsville (37 terminations) does not independently trigger WARN coverage** — a point plaintiff's own data workbook obscures with an apparent spreadsheet error; the **faltering company and unforeseeable business circumstances exceptions present genuine disputes of material fact** that cannot be resolved on summary judgment, particularly given evidence that the triggering events (the Ridgewater denial on January 8 and the FreshPath collapse on January 10, 2025) occurred only days before the terminations; and plaintiff's **damages figure is inflated and legally erroneous** because it ignores statutory offsets and relies on an average daily pay figure inconsistent with the underlying payroll data. At minimum, the Court should deny summary judgment on exceptions and damages; at best, plaintiff's motion may be defeated in substantial part on the Huntsville claims and on the exceptions.

## II. Factual Background (Record Facts)

- Greenleaf employed 644 full-time employees across three plants: Nashville (312), Chattanooga (189), Huntsville (143). (Headcount Data; SUMF ¶¶ 5–8.)
- SunHarvest (31% of revenue, ~$57.97M) gave written non-renewal notice on October 22, 2024. (Voss Dep. 16:14–23.)
- Voss "suspected" non-renewal as early as September 2024, but SunHarvest had made similar noises in 2021 and 2022 and renewed both times; no written indication of non-renewal existed before October 22. (Voss Dep. 15:3–16:3.)
- Mitigation efforts: FreshPath LOI (Dec. 5, 2024, ~$22M, non-binding) — collapsed Jan. 10, 2025; Ridgewater $15M credit line (applied Dec. 12, 2024) — denied Jan. 8, 2025; preliminary talks with Harmon Grocers (no LOI); single introductory meeting with Blue Ridge Co-op (Dec. 18, 2024).
- Cash position Jan. 1, 2025: ~$8.2M against ~$14.6M monthly operating costs — under one month of runway. (Chen Dep.)
- Pinnacle report delivered Dec. 20, 2024 (recommending 247 position eliminations); Board approved RIF Jan. 3, 2025; terminations executed Jan. 17, 2025 (Nashville 138; Chattanooga 72) and Jan. 24, 2025 (Huntsville 37, delayed to complete a short-term contract).
- No WARN notice was given to any employee, the State of Tennessee, the State of Alabama, or any local government unit. (Greenleaf Answer ¶ 23; Kowalski Dep.)
- HR VP Kowalski raised WARN compliance in mid-November 2024 and December 19, 2024, and prepared a draft notice template in late November; she was directed to "hold off" pending business development outcomes.

## III. Weaknesses in Plaintiff's Motion and Recommended Opposition Strategies

### A. Coverage / Threshold Issues

**1. Huntsville does not independently trigger WARN coverage.**
Under 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(3), a mass layoff requires an employment loss of at least 50 employees at a *single site* (with the 33% test) or 500+ employees. Huntsville had only **37** terminations out of 143 (25.9%) — below both the 50-employee floor and the 33% threshold. Our Answer (¶ 31) already preserves this defense.

*Notably, plaintiff's own Exhibit (Headcount Data, Plant Summary tab) contains an apparent error: it lists Huntsville as "Meets 50+ Terminated Threshold: Yes (37 ≥ 50)" — an arithmetically false statement on the face of plaintiff's own exhibit.* Use this affirmatively to attack the reliability of plaintiff's data presentation.

**Strategy:** Move to strike or dispute the Huntsville rows of the Headcount Data exhibit; argue affirmatively that the Huntsville terminations are not WARN-covered unless aggregated. This eliminates 37 of 247 claimants and, if the single-site theory fails, materially shrinks the case.

**2. The "single site of employment" aggregation theory is legally weak — but beware its double edge.**
Plaintiff's alternative theory (all three plants as one site) contradicts 20 C.F.R. § 639.3(i)(2): the plants are in *different cities and states*, are not in "reasonable geographic proximity," do not share staff or equipment, and have separate workforces and supervisors. Centralized HR/payroll alone does not merge separate physical facilities into a single site; § 639.3(i)(2) requires geographic proximity *and* shared staff/equipment.

**Caution — strategic tension:** Aggregation actually *hurts* plaintiff on Huntsville (37 alone fails; 247 aggregated passes), but plaintiff pleads it only "in the alternative." Conversely, if we prevail on separate sites, we lose aggregation but win Huntsville outright. Also note: if aggregation were accepted, it helps plaintiff only if the two January termination dates fall within a 90-day look-back under § 2101(a)(3)(B) — which they do (Jan. 17 and Jan. 24), so do not rest on the one-week stagger alone. However, we should still develop evidence that the staggering was operationally driven (Voss Dep. 38:12–40:4; Kowalski Dep. 80:7–11) to rebut any manipulation narrative and to support the separate-site argument that each plant's terminations reflected local operational realities.

**3. No plant closing occurred.**
Voss testified all three plants remain operational today with reduced workforces (Voss Dep. 32:8–23). Plaintiff's suggestion that Nashville was a "de facto plant closing" (Motion § I.B) is unsupported; a 44.2% RIF at a still-operating facility is a mass layoff, not a closing. Concede nothing; this narrows the issues.

### B. The Exceptions — The Heart of the Opposition

**4. Unforeseeable business circumstances (29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(2)(A); 20 C.F.R. § 639.9) presents a genuine dispute of material fact.**
Key record support:
- The October 22, 2024 letter was the **first written communication** of non-renewal (Voss Dep. 16:20–17:3: "That's when I knew it was real").
- SunHarvest had made identical "exploring alternatives" noises in 2021 and 2022 and **renewed both times** (Voss Dep. 15:20–16:5) — a reasonable businessperson could treat October 22 as an abrupt, unanticipated blow from a decade-long, annually renewed relationship.
- The critical *sudden* events were the **Ridgewater denial (Jan. 8)** and the **FreshPath collapse (Jan. 10)** — days before the January 17 terminations. The loss of both the bridge capital and the principal replacement customer within 48 hours is a textbook "sudden, dramatic, unexpected action or condition outside the employer's control."
- Plaintiff's foreseeability argument — that 87/94 days elapsed since October 22 — conflates *foreseeability of a business problem* with *foreseeability that a mass layoff of this scope at this time would occur*. Until Pinnacle's December 20 report, the scope of any RIF was unknown (Kowalski Dep. 148:1–11: numbers "weren't finalized until late December"); Kowalski testified the situation was "very fluid" and that if FreshPath had closed, layoffs might have been avoided at one or two plants (Kowalski Dep. 150:4–7).

**Weakness we must confront:** Voss's September 2024 "suspicion" testimony and the Kowalski testimony that by "early November" layoffs were a question of "how many and where" (Kowalski Dep. 30:14–18) cut against unforeseeability. Also, the employer bears the burden on exceptions, and courts scrutinize them strictly. **Reframe:** the exception need not excuse the entire 60 days to defeat summary judgment — the *reduced* notice attributable to the January 8/10 events is itself a jury question. A factual dispute over foreseeability alone defeats Rule 56.

**5. Faltering company exception (29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(1); 20 C.F.R. § 639.8) also presents genuine disputes.**
Elements: (i) company actively seeking capital/business reasonably expected to avoid the layoff; (ii) company reasonably and in good faith believed notice could be postponed or the layoff averted; (iii) if a viable offer was refused, refusal was prudent.
- **Actively seeking:** FreshPath LOI (signed), Ridgewater application, Harmon Grocers and Blue Ridge outreach. (Voss Dep. 28–31; Kowalski Dep. 91–95.)
- **Reasonable/good-faith belief:** Voss testified she genuinely believed FreshPath would close and hoped to scale back or avoid layoffs (Voss Dep. 29:3–6, 31:7–12; Kowalski Dep. 68:7–10: if FreshPath closed, "only 100 or 120 positions" might have been cut).
- **The clear-record problem:** plaintiff cites Chen's testimony that FreshPath's $22M would replace only ~38% of the lost SunHarvest revenue (SUMF ¶ 21) and Kowalski's December 19 statement that "even if FreshPath came through, we'd still need to do significant layoffs" (Kowalski Dep. 93:14–18). This is our strongest exposure on the "reasonably and in good faith" element.
  **Counter-strategy:** (a) Kowalski herself testified that with FreshPath the layoffs could have been reduced enough to *avoid WARN coverage at one or two plants* (Kowalski Dep. 150:4–7) — a belief that the *scope and WARN-triggering character* of the layoff could be affected is sufficient to create a fact dispute; (b) the Ridgewater line of credit was pursued as bridge capital, an independent statutory path; (c) good faith is quintessentially a jury question. Supplement with a Chen declaration emphasizing the genuine prospect of FreshPath consummation and the credit-line strategy.
- **Legal note:** The faltering company exception cannot excuse a *complete* failure to give *any* notice once layoffs became unavoidable (plaintiff's Section II.C). But under § 639.8/§ 639.9, an employer invoking an exception must give as much notice as practicable. **We must be prepared to show that after January 10, terminations within seven days were as practicable as possible** — Chen's testimony that the company had "weeks, not months" of operating cash (Kowalski Dep. 155:7–10; Chen Dep. 63) supports this.

**6. The "hold off on notices" evidence and privilege fights.**
Kowalski's testimony that she raised WARN in mid-November, was told to "hold off," disagreed, and drafted notices by November 25–26 (Kowalski Dep. 47–52, 161–163) is the single worst fact in the record. Plaintiff uses it to show deliberate non-compliance and to defeat both exceptions (bad faith).

**Strategies:**
- Emphasize that the "hold off" direction came *after consultation with outside counsel* (Kowalski Dep. 47:14–19) — Greenleaf acted with legal advice; the substance of that advice is privileged and, per our instruction at deposition, remains protected. Assert privilege against any use of the "advice of counsel" context; if plaintiff presses, seek a *de facto* waiver ruling in our favor (we have not affirmatively pleaded advice of counsel as a defense — the Answer ¶ 25 references good faith "with the advice of legal counsel," which is a waiver risk; **consider whether to amend or cabin that pleading**).
- Reframe the timeline: in mid-November the *scope* of layoffs was unknown (Kowalski Dep. 148), so no notice obligation had yet attached; premature notice of a layoff that might not occur (or might be far smaller) would itself have harmed employees and violated the Act's premise of notice of a *reasonably foreseeable* employment loss. Kowalski's own testimony that premature notification causes disruption and can be "self-fulfilling" (Kowalski Dep. 152) helps.
- On the math point (notices sent November 15 would have achieved compliance — Kowalski Dep. 163:1–8): argue hindsight; the legally relevant question is foreseeability of a WARN-triggering layoff *at that time*, not after the fact.

### C. Damages

**7. Plaintiff's damages calculation is inflated and legally erroneous.**
- Plaintiff's average daily rate of **$329.47** (SUMF ¶ 31) is inconsistent with plaintiff's own Termination Detail data, where most daily rates cluster between roughly $110 and $260 (e.g., Delaney $258.90; Briggs $186.85; most production employees $115–$150). The $329.47 average appears unsupported and should be challenged with a corrected computation from the same data set. (Note: the SUMF cites a "Payroll Summary Tab" — verify against the Termination Detail; the discrepancy itself is a genuine dispute.)
- Damages under 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(1) are **subject to offset** for interim earnings and, per most courts, voluntary severance and other compensation during the violation period. Note the internal conflict in plaintiff's record: Kowalski Dep. 78:9–12 mentions employees were given "severance information packets," while SUMF ¶ 32 asserts no severance was paid — develop discovery on what, if anything, was actually paid.
- Each affected employee's damages must be calculated individually (actual final average regular rate, offsets, mitigation) — an individualized inquiry that defeats summary judgment on aggregate damages and supports our Rule 23 predominance arguments.
- Damages for the 37 Huntsville employees should be deferred entirely pending resolution of the coverage issue (§ A.1 above).

### D. Procedural and Evidentiary Issues

**8. Local Rule 56.01 response.** File a paragraph-by-paragraph response to the SUMF, admitting only the genuinely undisputed facts (headcounts, dates, zero notice) and disputing every characterization ("foreseeable," "deliberately chose," "ample time," single-site assertions, damages figures). Many SUMF paragraphs are argument dressed as fact.

**9. Attack plaintiff's exhibits:**
- The Headcount Data Plant Summary tab's Huntsville "Yes (37 ≥ 50)" entry is facially false; consider a motion to strike or at least a pointed footnote.
- The Briggs Declaration contains inadmissible content: ¶ 12 (legal conclusion that Greenleaf "violated the WARN Act"), ¶ 13 (hearsay from "at least a dozen" unnamed coworkers regarding notice), ¶ 7 (hearsay from supervisor Henley; note also it shows employees already rumored the customer loss — mildly helpful to us on the rumor-of-notice point but inadmissible for truth), ¶¶ 10–11 (individual hardship irrelevant to summary judgment on liability). Object under Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c)(4) and FRE 801/802/701.
- Note: Plaintiff's SUMF cites a "Declaration of Marcus Delaney" — confirm it is actually in the record; if not filed, object to facts relying on it (SUMF ¶¶ 4, 26).
- SUMF ¶ 21 relies on Chen deposition testimony characterizing FreshPath's adequacy — dispute as argumentative.

**10. Partial judgment slicing.** Even if the Court grants liability as to Nashville and Chattanooga (where thresholds are plainly met and zero notice is admitted), argue that (a) the exceptions go to trial; (b) the duration of the violation period (how much notice was "practicable") is a jury question requiring individualized proof; and (c) Huntsville and damages are not properly within any grant of partial summary judgment. Framing: plaintiff seeks judgment on a *claim*, but the merits of our affirmative defenses are indivisible from liability — a movant cannot obtain summary judgment while ignoring the nonmoving party's supported affirmative defenses.

**11. Class certification synergy.** Individualized damages, mitigation, offsets, the Huntsville coverage question, and state-specific law (Tennessee Act does not reach Huntsville — Answer ¶ 47) all feed the Rule 23 predominance opposition. Build the record here for use there.

## V. Candid Risk Assessment

The gravest exposures are (i) the admitted zero notice for 138 Nashville and 72 Chattanooga employees at plants that independently meet mass-layoff thresholds, and (ii) the "hold off" testimony with the November notice math. Summary judgment denial on the exceptions and damages is realistically achievable; outright victory on liability for Nashville/Chattanooga is unlikely. Realistic best outcomes: dismissal of the Huntsville class members from the WARN count, trial on exceptions with a reduced violation period, and sharply reduced damages with offsets.
