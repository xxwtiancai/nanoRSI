## I. Executive Summary

Plaintiff moves for partial summary judgment on liability under the federal WARN Act, resting on four pillars: (1) coverage thresholds are met at each facility (or alternatively at a combined "single site"); (2) zero days of notice was provided (admitted); (3) the "unforeseeable business circumstances" (UBC) exception fails because Greenleaf had 87–94 days of warning after the October 22, 2024 SunHarvest non-renewal letter; and (4) the "faltering company" exception fails because Greenleaf's mitigation efforts were speculative and belated.

The motion is not as airtight as it appears. There are genuine disputes of material fact on foreseeability and on the faltering-company defense, a threshold coverage defect as to Huntsville (37 terminations at a concededly separate single site), analytical errors in Plaintiff's own data exhibit, and evidentiary weaknesses. Our opposition should (a) contest the SUMF paragraph-by-paragraph to defeat the "undisputed facts" premise, (b) develop both statutory exceptions with concrete financial and timeline evidence, and (c) contain damages exposure by litigating offsets and severance even if liability is found.

**Candid assessment:** The core facts — 644 employees, 247 terminations, zero notice, and internal awareness of WARN obligations by mid-November — are largely admitted or established by our own witnesses. The realistic strategic goal is to defeat summary judgment and preserve the exceptions for trial, while narrowing class-wide exposure (particularly Huntsville and damages offsets).

## II. Principal Weaknesses in Plaintiff's Motion

### A. Threshold / Coverage Arguments

1. **Huntsville does not independently trigger WARN coverage.** Huntsville lost 37 of 143 employees (25.9%) — below both the 50-employee minimum and the 33% threshold under 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(3). Our Answer (¶ 31) correctly denies an independent trigger. Plaintiff's only route to Huntsville is aggregation via a "single site of employment" theory.

2. **The single-site aggregation theory is legally weak.** Three facilities in different cities in two states, with distinct workforces and no shared commuting, are paradigmatic *separate* single sites under 20 C.F.R. § 639.3(i)(2). That regulation's multi-building rule requires geographic proximity plus shared staff and equipment; centralized HR/payroll at corporate headquarters (Kowalski Dep. 13) is ordinary corporate administration, not worksite integration. Plaintiff's cited cases (*Teamsters Local 413*; *Rifkin*) are out-of-circuit and address different configurations.

3. **Errors and advocacy embedded in Plaintiff's own Exhibit (Headcount Data).** The Plant Summary tab states Huntsville "Meets 50+ Terminated Threshold: **Yes (37 ≥ 50)**" and "Meets 33% Workforce Threshold: **Yes (25.9% ≥ 33%)**" — both facially false. Plaintiff's own summary chart misstates the statutory thresholds, and the "WARN Act Triggered (Plaintiff's Position)" column concedes these are argumentative characterizations, not data. Attack the exhibit's reliability and oppose any SUMF paragraph relying on it as to Huntsville.

4. **No plant closing occurred.** Voss testified all three plants remain operational with reduced workforces (Voss Dep. 32:8–33:5; Nashville now ~174). Plaintiff's "de facto plant closing" suggestion for Nashville (Mot. § I.B) is unsupported and contradicted by this testimony.

### B. The "87 Days" Foreseeability Argument (Motion § II.A) — Contestable

5. **The relevant trigger date is disputed, not October 22.** Voss testified SunHarvest had threatened non-renewal in 2021 and 2022 and always renewed (Voss Dep. 15:3–25:5); the September 2024 comments were "similar" to prior years; nothing in writing until October 22 ("That's when I knew it was real," Voss Dep. 16:23). A reasonable factfinder could find the customer loss was not reasonably foreseeable until October 22, and the *scale and necessity* of layoffs was not foreseeable until the Pinnacle report (Dec. 20) and the sequential collapse of Ridgewater (Jan. 8) and FreshPath (Jan. 10). Foreseeability is a fact question ill-suited to summary judgment.

6. **Even accepting October 22, foreseeability of layoffs *of this scope* remained unresolved.** Kowalski testified on cross that in mid-November the scope was unknown and numbers were not finalized until late December (Kowalski Dep. 148, 150); if FreshPath had closed, layoffs might have been reduced to 100–120 or avoided at one or two plants (Kowalski Dep. 68, 150). This supports the UBC exception and the reasonableness of not issuing premature, potentially false notices.

7. **The UBC exception does not require perfect prescience.** It asks whether the circumstances causing the layoff were reasonably foreseeable when notice would have been due, and § 2102(b) requires only "as much notice as is practicable." The sudden loss of 31% of revenue ($57.97M) from a customer that had always renewed is a classic UBC scenario, consistent with the company's contemporaneous pursuit of FreshPath, Ridgewater, Harmon Grocers, and Blue Ridge Co-op.

### C. Faltering Company Exception (Motion § II.B) — Genuine Fact Disputes Exist

8. **The financial facts support the defense.** As of January 1, 2025: $8.2M cash against $14.6M monthly operating costs (Chen Dep. per SUMF ¶ 10; Kowalski Dep. 95) — less than one month of runway. The company actively sought credit ($15M Ridgewater application, Dec. 12) and replacement revenue (FreshPath LOI, Dec. 5) in the reasonable hope of avoiding layoffs; both failed within a 72-hour window (Jan. 8 and Jan. 10), one week before the first terminations. Whether these efforts were "reasonably likely" to avert the layoff and pursued "as soon as practicable" are quintessential jury questions.

9. **Kowalski's testimony corroborates good-faith pursuit.** Her December 19 call with Voss — FreshPath "still alive," layoffs "might be much smaller or might not happen at all" (Kowalski Dep. 51) — and her testimony that "everything accelerated" when FreshPath fell through (Kowalski Dep. 155) support a genuine, contemporaneous belief that layoffs were avoidable.

10. **Plaintiff's strongest counter-evidence must be neutralized, not ignored:**
    - **Chen's testimony that FreshPath's $22M would replace only ~38% of lost revenue** (SUMF ¶ 21) undercuts "reasonably likely to avoid." Response: replacing 38% of the gap could have materially reduced the scope (Kowalski Dep. 68: "maybe only cut 100 or 120"); the exception requires only a realistic prospect of avoiding or postponing layoffs, not full replacement. Note: no Chen deposition excerpts are among the motion's supporting materials — demand the cited pages and oppose reliance on testimony not before the Court.
    - **Kowalski: "even if FreshPath came through, we'd still need to do significant layoffs"** (Kowalski Dep. 93). Our single worst fact. Contextualize: she also testified FreshPath could have spared entire plants; her December statement goes to *scale*, not to the existence of a triggering event.
    - **The mid-November "hold off" instruction** (Kowalski Dep. 49–50) and Voss's admission that no notices were sent even in the 14 days after Board approval (Voss Dep. 34) damage the "as soon as practicable" element. Frame the delay as tied to genuine uncertainty (FreshPath, Ridgewater) and legal advice of counsel; litigate privilege carefully (see § III.3).

### D. Damages and Procedural Weaknesses

11. **The "zero severance" premise is internally contradicted.** SUMF ¶ 32 asserts no employee received severance; but Kowalski testified Nashville employees were "given severance information packets" (Kowalski Dep. 78:10–11), and Briggs Decl. ¶ 5 confirms employees received severance information and final-paycheck instructions. Any severance actually paid is offsettable under 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(2)/(b). Discovery on actual payments is essential.

12. **Damages are overstated and ignore offsets.** The ~$4.88M figure (247 × $329.47 avg. daily rate × 60, SUMF ¶¶ 31–32) assumes a full 60-day violation period and gross rates for all 247 employees. Must be reduced by interim earnings/mitigation, unemployment compensation, benefits received, and voluntary payments (Answer ¶ 51). Individualized mitigation issues also support Rule 23(b)(3) predominance arguments (Answer ¶ 56).

13. **SUMF defects — several paragraphs are not "undisputed":**
    - ¶ 8 ("suspected as early as September") — Voss's quote is taken out of context; her full testimony describes prior-year renewal patterns and no written warning until October 22 (Voss Dep. 15–16).
    - ¶ 14 ("no efforts to provide conditional or partial notice") — contradicts Kowalski's late-November draft notice template and data preparation (Kowalski Dep. 51, 153); efforts short of sending notice were made.
    - ¶¶ 22–25 percentages/totals draw on plaintiff's flawed Headcount Exhibit (see § II.A.3).
    - ¶¶ 28–30 (centralized management as aggregation evidence) are legal conclusions, not facts; central administration does not make distinct worksites a single site.
    Under L.R. 56.01 and *Anderson/Celotex*, each contested paragraph must be met with record citations; failure risks deemed admission.

14. **Excerpted depositions.** Voss (selective excerpts from 73 pages) and Kowalski (plaintiff-selected excerpts from 214 pages) — move to strike or supplement under Rule 56(e)/32 where excerpts omit context (e.g., Voss pp. 15–16 on renewal history; Kowalski cross at 148–155).

## III. Recommended Opposition Strategy

1. **Primary theme: this is a fact case on both exceptions.** Foreseeability (UBC) and the "reasonably likely / as soon as practicable" elements of the faltering-company defense are quintessential jury questions; the 87-day figure is a red herring because foreseeability of the layoff-triggering circumstances and scope remained unresolved until January 8–10, 2025.

2. **Attack Huntsville aggregation head-on.** Argue three separate single sites; Huntsville (37/143) triggers nothing federally; the Tennessee Act likewise does not reach Huntsville (Answer ¶ 47). This removes 37 putative class members from the federal claim regardless of outcome.

3. **Privilege and advice-of-counsel.** The Kowalski "hold off" testimony came in over our privilege objection. Move to strike the instruction-dependent testimony (Kowalski Dep. 49); if plaintiff presses the "conscious decision to withhold notice" narrative, consider selectively invoking advice-of-counsel (accepting subject-matter waiver) to reframe the timeline.

4. **Build the evidentiary record:**
    - Declarations from Voss (renewal history; genuine hope in FreshPath; operational reasons for the Huntsville one-week stagger — the short-term distributor contract) and Chen (cash runway, Ridgewater denial rationale, FreshPath viability).
    - Pinnacle Report, board minutes (Nov. 4, Nov. 8, Jan. 3), FreshPath LOI, Ridgewater denial letter — all show diligence and contemporaneous good faith.
    - Rebut the "de facto plant closing" theory with current operating headcounts (~174/117/106, Voss Dep. 33).

5. **Damages containment track (parallel to liability opposition):**
    - Discovery/supplementation on severance actually paid (contradiction between SUMF ¶ 32 and Kowalski 78 / Briggs ¶ 5).
    - Per-employee mitigation and interim-earnings data; unemployment offsets.
    - Note civil penalties under § 2104(a)(3) run only to units of local government.

6. **Procedural asks in the opposition brief:**
    - Deny the motion or, at minimum, deny as to Huntsville and as to both exceptions.
    - Strike or disregard the Headcount Exhibit's "threshold" columns as false characterizations of data.
    - Require plaintiff to file the full Chen deposition or limit reliance on SUMF citations to it; supplement or strike context-stripped deposition excerpts.
    - If needed, a Rule 56(d) declaration regarding outstanding severance/mitigation discovery.

7. **Class-certification synergy.** Individualized damages, mitigation, and Huntsville coverage issues feed directly into our Rule 23 predominance opposition (Answer ¶¶ 55–57). Coordinate briefing.

## IV. Candid Risk Assessment

- **Nashville (138/312, 44.2%) and Chattanooga (72/189, 38.1%) independently trigger the WARN Act as mass layoffs at separate single sites; this is effectively uncontestable.** Zero notice is admitted (Answer ¶ 23). Liability exposure as to 210 employees is real.
- The best realistic summary-judgment outcome is **denial in full** on the exceptions; a partial loss (liability as to Nashville/Chattanooga) is plausible. Even a successful exception defense typically *shortens* rather than eliminates the notice period (notice "as soon as practicable"), so partial damages remain triable.
- The Kowalski "hold off" testimony and the December 19 email are plaintiff's best trial exhibits; prepare Voss and Kowalski explanations grounded in genuine business uncertainty, not cost avoidance.
