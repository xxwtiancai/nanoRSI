## I. Executive Summary

Plaintiff moves for partial summary judgment on the federal WARN Act claim, relying on an unusually strong admitted record: Greenleaf has admitted it terminated 247 employees (138 Nashville / 72 Chattanooga on January 17, 2025; 37 Huntsville on January 24, 2025) with **zero days' notice** to employees or any government unit (Answer ¶¶ 19–23). The defense therefore cannot win on liability facts; the opposition must succeed on **legal thresholds (single site of employment)** and on **genuine disputes of material fact regarding the statutory exceptions** — chiefly the "unforeseeable business circumstances" and "faltering company" exceptions, both pleaded as affirmative defenses.

Realistic objectives, in descending order of importance:

1. **Defeat summary judgment entirely** by demonstrating genuine factual disputes on foreseeability, the timing of the layoff decision, and active pursuit of capital/revenue that could have averted or narrowed the layoff.
2. **Cabin damages exposure** by contesting aggregation of the three plants — Huntsville (37 of 143; 25.9%) does **not** independently trigger WARN as a mass layoff, and the "single site of employment" theory is legally weak for facilities in three cities/two states.
3. **Preserve offsets and mitigation defenses** on damages, and challenge the $329.47/day average and the full 60-day violation calculation.

## II. Weaknesses in Plaintiff's Motion

### A. The Single-Site / Aggregation Theory Is Plaintiff's Softest Point

1. **Huntsville does not independently trigger WARN.** A mass layoff requires ≥50 employees **and** ≥33% of the workforce at a *single site*. Huntsville: 37 of 143 (25.9%) fails both prongs. Greenleaf's Answer (¶¶ 30–31) correctly contests this; the headcount data confirms the numbers.
2. **Plaintiff's "single site of employment" alternative theory is a stretch.** Under 20 C.F.R. § 639.3(i), separate facilities are separate sites; the three plants are not "in reasonable geographic proximity" (three cities, two states), do not share the same staff and equipment, and no employees commute between them. Centralized HR/payroll alone does not merge separate production plants into one "single site" — if centralized management sufficed, virtually every multi-plant manufacturer would be a single site, contrary to the regulation's structure. Plaintiff's cited cases (*Teamsters Local 413*; *Rifkin*) do not hold that centralized HR converts three plants in two states into one site.
3. **Plant-closing framing fails.** Plaintiff floats a "de facto plant closing" theory for Nashville, but Voss testified all three plants remain operational today with reduced workforces (Voss Dep. 32:8–33:5). No facility shut down.
4. **Strategic tension for plaintiff.** Plaintiff pleads single-site aggregation only *in the alternative*; aggregation is needed only for Huntsville and is legally weak — reducing the class by 37 employees and undercutting the "unified 247-person layoff" narrative.

### B. Genuine Disputes of Material Fact on the Unforeseeable Business Circumstances Exception (29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(2)(A))

5. **Foreseeability is measured when notice would have been due, and the triggering circumstance was the non-renewal itself.** Voss testified SunHarvest threatened non-renewal in 2021 and 2022 and always renewed (Voss Dep. 15:1–25); the October 22, 2024 letter was the first written indication (Voss Dep. 16:20–17:3). Her September "suspicion" — based on verbal comments materially identical to prior years' renewals — does not establish that non-renewal of the largest customer was *reasonably foreseeable*.
6. **Even accepting October 22 as the trigger, the scale and timing of the layoffs were not fixed until late in the period.** Kowalski confirmed on cross that in mid-November the scope was unknown (Kowalski Dep. 148:4–11) and the situation remained "very fluid" through mid-December (150:8–10). The Pinnacle report (Dec. 20) and Board approval (Jan. 3) fixed the scope; the FreshPath LOI (Dec. 5) genuinely could have reduced the RIF to 100–120 positions or eliminated layoffs at one or two plants (Kowalski Dep. 68:8–10, 150:4–7). Whether a reasonably prudent employer, at the time 60-day notice would have been due, could have foreseen a mass layoff of this scope is a quintessential jury question.
7. **The 87/94-day arithmetic is a legal non-sequitur.** Plaintiff counts from the SunHarvest letter to termination, but the WARN clock runs from when the employer *ordered* the layoff, not from receipt of bad news. The Board ordered the RIF January 3 (arguably contingent on FreshPath — Kowalski Dep. 155:4–5); FreshPath collapsed January 10; terminations occurred 7 days later. The operative unforeseeable events — the Ridgewater credit denial (Jan. 8) and the FreshPath collapse (Jan. 10) — occurred days before the layoffs, squarely within the exception's design.
8. **Caution:** Voss's September "suspicion" (Voss Dep. 15:3) and the 87-day gap are plaintiff's best sound bites. The opposition must reframe: suspicion of *possible* non-renewal ≠ foreseeability that a 247-person mass layoff at these plants would be ordered, particularly where replacement revenue and bridge credit were actively in play.

### C. Genuine Disputes on the Faltering Company Exception (§ 2102(b)(1))

9. The record supports each element as a disputed factual issue:
   - FreshPath LOI (Dec. 5, ~$22M) — executed; collapsed Jan. 10 (Voss Dep. 28–29).
   - Ridgewater $15M credit application (Dec. 12) — denied Jan. 8 (Voss Dep. 30).
   - Harmon Grocers and Blue Ridge Co-op outreach (Voss Dep. 29–30).
   - Acute distress: $8.2M cash against $14.6M monthly burn as of Jan. 1 (Chen Dep.; SUMF ¶ 10; Kowalski Dep. 95:3–7).
10. **Pre-empt plaintiff's two counterarguments:**
    - *FreshPath replaced only 38% of SunHarvest revenue (SUMF ¶ 21; Chen Dep. 58:2–14).* Response: the exception requires only a reasonable, good-faith belief that the efforts would avert or postpone the shutdown — which Kowalski's testimony (layoffs might shrink to 100–120 or be avoided at some plants) directly supports. Whether that belief was reasonable given a ~$36M shortfall is a jury question.
    - *Notice must be given "as soon as practicable" even under an exception* (Motion § II.C) — our most vulnerable flank; see Section III, Item 2.

### D. Damages Overreach

11. **Full 60-day damages assumed.** Plaintiff assumes a 60-day violation period per employee ($329.47/day average; ~$4.88M total). Under § 2104, damages are tied to the notice shortfall; if any exception applies, liability is reduced or eliminated. Damages should not be resolved on this motion in any event.
12. **Offsets and mitigation.** The Answer (¶¶ 50–51) reserves offsets for interim earnings, unemployment compensation, and benefits. Briggs found comparable work in ~3 months, illustrating individualized mitigation issues — which also undermines class-wide damages and supports Rule 23 objections.
13. **Huntsville exclusion.** If aggregation fails, 37 Huntsville employees fall out of the federal WARN class (and are outside the Tennessee Act per Answer ¶ 47).
14. **Benefits component unquantified.** The damages framework lumps in medical/benefit costs without record support; demand per-employee proof.

### E. Evidentiary and Procedural Attacks

15. **Argumentative SUMF characterizations.** Several SUMF paragraphs embed conclusions (e.g., ¶ 14 "no efforts"; "deliberately chose not to" in the motion) and cite declarations containing hearsay and legal conclusions (Briggs Decl. ¶¶ 8, 12–13 — coworker statements, "violated the WARN Act"). Respond paragraph-by-paragraph per Local Rule 56.01 with admissions, denials, and additional material facts.
16. **Selective excerpting.** Plaintiff designated excerpts of a 214-page Kowalski transcript; designate the full cross-examination passages (fluidity, premature-notice risks, "self-fulfilling prophecy" testimony at 152) and counter-designate Chen and Voss excerpts.
17. **Privilege issue.** Kowalski's testimony about the mid-November Ashford conversation skirts attorney-client privilege; plaintiff uses the *fact* of her warning as foreseeability evidence. The "hold off" direction is already in the record — contain further testimony where possible.

## III. Our Weaknesses (Candid Assessment)

1. **Zero notice is admitted** (Answer ¶ 23; Voss Dep. 39–40; Kowalski Dep. 52). There is no factual defense to the notice failure itself; everything turns on the exceptions.
2. **"As soon as practicable" problem.** Even where an exception applies, the employer must give as much notice as practicable. The record shows 14 days between Board approval (Jan. 3) and the Nashville/Chattanooga terminations, **and 7 days between FreshPath's collapse (Jan. 10) and the terminations** — with no notice in either window (Voss Dep. 34; Kowalski Dep. 64). Worse, Greenleaf knew by January 17 that Huntsville terminations would occur January 24 and told those employees nothing (Voss Dep. 39) — a week of certain, imminent layoffs with no notice. Mitigation: Kowalski's testimony that the Jan. 3 approval was contingent on FreshPath (155:4–5) and that operational concerns drove the sequence; but expect this argument to limit, not eliminate, our exceptions.
3. **Kowalski's "hold off" testimony and her December 19 email** flagging WARN obligations — combined with her admission that notices sent mid-November would have achieved compliance (Kowalski Dep. 163:5–8) — are plaintiff's strongest foreseeability/deliberate-delay evidence. We must contextualize: scope uncertainty, genuine pursuit of alternatives, good-faith belief the RIF might shrink or be averted.
4. **Bad-optics facts:** a supervisor told Briggs "everything is fine" in early December (Briggs Decl. ¶ 7); the company relied on Huntsville employees' work through their final week while concealing their termination; no severance was paid (SUMF ¶ 32), eliminating any voluntary-payment offset and compounding the equities.
5. **Voss's September suspicion plus the 87-day math** will dominate any hearing. Anchor foreseeability to the *decision to order the RIF*, not the loss of the customer.

## IV. Recommended Opposition Strategy

1. **File a timely opposition, a paragraph-by-paragraph response to the SUMF, and a statement of additional material facts** under Local Rule 56.01, citing the cross-examination testimony (fluidity, premature-notice risks, contingent Board approval, financial distress).
2. **Lead with the single-site argument as to Huntsville.** Force plaintiff to elect a theory; argue aggregation fails under 20 C.F.R. § 639.3(i) (separate cities/states, no shared staff/equipment, no commuting). Cleanest partial win available: 37 employees out of the federal class.
3. **Build the exceptions narrative around the decision timeline:** Sept.–Oct. ambiguity (prior-year bluff renewals) → Oct. 22 non-renewal → immediate, documented mitigation (FreshPath LOI, Ridgewater application, Harmon, Blue Ridge) → Pinnacle report Dec. 20 → contingent Board approval Jan. 3 → credit denial Jan. 8, FreshPath collapse Jan. 10 → terminations Jan. 17/24. Frame the operative unforeseeable circumstances as the January collapse of all mitigation, days before the RIF.
4. **Argue foreseeability and the reasonableness/good-faith elements of both exceptions are jury questions unsuited to summary judgment**, assessed from the employer's circumstances at the time notice was due.
5. **Address "as soon as practicable" head-on:** concede the jury may reduce damages for the Jan. 10–17 window, but argue that at most shortens the violation period — it does not eliminate the exceptions or justify judgment for plaintiff.
6. **Contest damages:** no summary adjudication of the 60-day period; individualized mitigation/offsets; benefits unquantified; Huntsville excluded; preserve Rule 23 challenges (individualized damages, site-specific thresholds) — class issues are not before the Court on this motion.
7. **Evidentiary practice:** object to hearsay and legal conclusions in the Briggs Declaration (¶¶ 8, 12–13); serve full transcripts and counter-designations (Chen, Voss, Kowalski); consider declarations from Chen (Ridgewater process, financial runway) and Voss (2021/2022 bluff-renewal history; operational reasons for the stagger).
8. **Settlement posture.** Given admitted zero notice, the Kowalski testimony, and the January 17→24 Huntsville week, the exceptions are viable but not certain. The Huntsville carve-out and mitigation offsets provide valuation leverage. Recommend a damages-focused mediation track parallel to the opposition, with exposure modeled at: (a) full class, 60 days (~$4.88M); (b) 210-employee Tennessee-only with notice-shortfall adjustment; (c) a settlement range reflecting exception risk to both sides.

---

## V. Conclusion

Plaintiff's motion is formidable on the admitted facts but vulnerable in three places: (1) the legally weak single-site aggregation needed to capture Huntsville; (2) genuine factual disputes on foreseeability and on the good-faith, actively-sought-capital elements of both statutory exceptions; and (3) premature, over-broad damages. The opposition should seek denial of summary judgment in full or, at worst, denial as to Huntsville and deferral of damages to trial.
