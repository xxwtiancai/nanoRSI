## I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

DataCore moves to dismiss all four counts of Pinnacle's Complaint on four grounds: (1) Rule 12(b)(1) — disputed amount in controversy; (2) Rule 12(b)(2) — lack of personal jurisdiction; (3) Rule 12(b)(3) — improper venue under the MSA's Travis County, Texas forum selection clause (§ 14.2); and (4) Rule 12(b)(6) — failure to state a claim, resting on the one-year contractual limitations period (§ 9.4), the liability cap (§ 11.1), the consequential damages waiver (§ 11.2), the integration clause (§ 14.7), and the Texas choice-of-law clause (§ 14.3).

**Bottom line:** The motion is vulnerable on nearly every front, but the forum selection clause is the most serious threat. Our strongest arguments are: (a) specific personal jurisdiction in Georgia is well pleaded (Atlanta office; Sousa's misrepresentations made from Georgia); (b) the fraudulent inducement claim is pleaded with particularity (who, what, when, where, why — the January 10, 2022 Sousa email, the February 14, 2022 SOW Capabilities Document, and DataCore CTO Subramanian's January 12, 2023 written admission); (c) a fraud claim challenging the *contract itself* as fraudulently induced undermines DataCore's ability to enforce the MSA's limitations period, cap, and damages waiver against that claim; and (d) DataCore's limitations argument turns on disputed "discovery" facts (when Pinnacle knew the *full extent* of the shortfall) improper for Rule 12. Note: DataCore moved under Rule 12(b)(3), but under *Atl. Marine* the proper vehicle is § 1404(a) transfer or forum non conveniens — not 12(b)(3) dismissal — an important procedural point in our favor.

## III. RULE 12(b)(1) — SUBJECT MATTER JURISDICTION / AMOUNT IN CONTROVERSY

**DataCore's argument:** Pinnacle's ~$4.47M damages figure is "speculative and inflated," particularly the $1.2M consequential damages (replacement system costs / lost business, per Greenleaf Accounting Group) and the Harmon Ridge Capital lending-harm allegations.

**Our position:**
- Complete diversity is undisputed (GA plaintiff; DE/TX defendant). The only attack is the amount in controversy.
- The legal test is whether it appears to a legal certainty that Pinnacle cannot recover ≥ $75,000 — a very low bar. Even DataCore's own math concedes Pinnacle paid **$931,000** in fees, and DataCore itself claims Pinnacle owes **$432,000** in unpaid fees. Reliance damages alone (fees paid) exceed the threshold by an order of magnitude.
- Damages quantification by an auditor (Greenleaf) is a merits question, not a jurisdictional one; amount-in-controversy attacks of this kind are routinely rejected where the claim is made in good faith.
- **Risk: Low.** This section of DataCore's brief is notably weak ("briefly notes").

## IV. RULE 12(b)(2) — PERSONAL JURISDICTION

**DataCore's argument (per Whitford Declaration):** DataCore is "at home" in Austin, Texas; servers are in Virginia and Oregon; support/engineering in Austin; suit does not arise from Georgia contacts.

**Our position:**
- **Specific jurisdiction is the centerpiece.** The Complaint pleads (¶¶ 12, 16, 25–44): DataCore maintains a permanent Atlanta office at 100 Techwood Drive NW staffed by ~14 employees; VP of Sales Victor Sousa is based in Atlanta; the alleged misrepresentations (Jan. 10, 2022 email; brochure delivered in person in Atlanta; SOW Capabilities Document sent from the Atlanta office; MSA signed by Sousa from Atlanta) were all made *from Georgia to a Georgia plaintiff*; implementation was performed for and injuries suffered in Georgia. Under *Burger King v. Rudzewicz* and Georgia's long-arm statute (O.C.G.A. § 9-10-91), these are purposeful contacts directly giving rise to the claims — a textbook prima facie case.
- **Credibility problem for DataCore:** The Whitford Declaration conspicuously *omits any mention of the Atlanta office or Sousa's location*, while describing Austin operations in detail. Declaration ¶ 12 also concedes negotiations occurred by "email and videoconference" with Southeast regional personnel. Silence is not denial; we should press this at the prima facie stage (jurisdictional discovery if needed).
- **General jurisdiction** (Complaint ¶ 17, alternative) is weaker post-*Daimler*; treat as fallback only.
- **Risk: Medium-Low** on specific jurisdiction. Recommend briefing *Burger King*/purposely-availed framework and requesting jurisdictional discovery in the alternative.

## V. RULE 12(b)(3) / FORUM SELECTION CLAUSE — THE MOST SERIOUS ISSUE

**DataCore's argument:** MSA § 14.2 mandates exclusive resolution in Travis County, Texas courts; under *Bremen/Stewart*, the clause is entitled to near-dispositive weight; Texas law (§ 14.3) governs.

**Our arguments:**

1. **Wrong procedural vehicle.** Under *Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court*, 571 U.S. 49 (2013), a forum selection clause pointing to another federal forum is enforced through **§ 1404(a) transfer or forum non conveniens**, not Rule 12(b)(3) dismissal. Venue here is otherwise proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)(2) (substantial events in N.D. Ga.). The motion should be denied on this ground alone or treated as a § 1404(a) motion — under which DataCore, as movant, bears the heavy burden of showing the public-interest factors overwhelmingly favor transfer.
2. **Fraudulent inducement scope argument.** Counts II–III attack the *contract as a whole* as fraudulently induced. A party should not be able to wield a forum clause contained in the very agreement alleged to be procured by fraud to strip the defrauded party of its chosen (and statutorily proper) forum. Case law is split, but the argument is available at minimum for the tort claims.
3. **Public-interest factors:** localized Georgia controversy, Georgia plaintiff, Georgia witnesses (Nance, Lofton, Holliday, Greenleaf, Harmon Ridge), Georgia UDTPA claim. Under *Atlantic Marine*, private-interest factors are deemed waived by the clause, but public factors (local interest, localized events) favor Georgia.
4. **Fallback:** even if the court enforces the clause as to contract-based claims, argue the fraud/misrepresentation claims fall outside or should proceed here pending resolution of inducement.

**Risk: Medium-High.** This is the issue most likely to succeed for DataCore in some form (transfer rather than dismissal). Prepare the § 1404(a) opposition arguments regardless of label.

## VI. RULE 12(b)(6) — COUNT-BY-COUNT ANALYSIS

### A. Count I — Breach of Contract

**DataCore's arguments and our responses:**

1. **One-year limitations period (§ 9.4)** — DataCore says Pinnacle "discovered" the breach by August 2022 (ADP complaints) and sued March 22, 2024.
   - *Response:* The contractual clock runs from when Pinnacle "knew or should have known of the facts giving rise to such claim." The full scope of breach — including the permanent incapacity of the platform — was not known until at least the **April 15, 2023 patch failed to cure** (still only 7,500 of 15,000 records). Filing March 22, 2024 is within one year of the failed remediation, and arguably within one year of Pinnacle's December 2023 continued-use period and January 15, 2024 termination. Discovery timing is a quintessential fact question improper for Rule 12. **Also:** DataCore's January 12, 2023 email induced reasonable reliance on a forthcoming fix, supporting equitable tolling/estoppel.
   - *Counter-risk:* If the court anchors on August 2022, the contract claim (unlike the fraud claims) may be time-barred. Mitigation: plead in the alternative that continuing breaches (monthly non-conforming performance under § 2.4(a)) restarted or continued the clock.

2. **Liability cap (§ 11.1, $432,000)** and **consequential damages waiver (§ 11.2)**:
   - These cap *contract* damages; they do not apply to the fraudulent inducement claim (Texas and Georgia law generally hold that a fraudfeasor cannot contractually limit liability for its own fraud, and unenforceability of exculpatory clauses against intentional conduct is well established). For Count I, argue the cap is at minimum a fact-bound damages issue not resolvable on a 12(b)(6) challenge to the *claim*; the cap limits remedies, not the existence of a claim, and dismissal is improper where damages exceed the cap.

### B. Count II — Fraudulent Inducement (our strongest count)

**DataCore's arguments and our responses:**

1. **Rule 9(b) particularity:** The Complaint pleads *who* (Sousa, Baines), *what* ("fully integrates with all major payroll platforms, including… ADP… out of the box with zero custom configuration"; "up to 15,000 concurrent temporary worker records with sub-second latency"; "native integration with 40+ payroll and HRIS platforms"), *when* (Jan. 10, 2022 email; Jan. 18 and Feb. 7, 2022 demos; Nov. 2021 brochure; Feb. 14, 2022 SOW Capabilities Document), *where* (from DataCore's Atlanta office), and *reliance and falsity* (4,000-record actual capacity; ~$85,000 undisclosed custom API work; staged demo environment). This comfortably satisfies 9(b) and *Iqbal/Twombly*.
2. **Integration clause (§ 14.7) / non-reliance:** DataCore argues § 14.7 forecloses reliance. *Response:* Under both Texas and Georgia law, a general integration/supersession clause does not bar fraudulent inducement claims absent a clear **non-reliance/disclaimer** provision negating justifiable reliance negotiated between sophisticated parties (cf. *Italian Colors* cited by DataCore is distinguishable — no explicit disclaimer here; § 14.7 contains no anti-reliance language). Moreover, § 14.7 superseded only representations "relating to such subject matter" *in the MSA's* exhibits — the SOW Capabilities Document was expressly *not* incorporated (Complaint ¶ 39).
3. **Knowledge/scienter:** Subramanian's Jan. 12, 2023 email — "performance limitations at scale that do not meet the capabilities described during the sales process" — is a direct corporate admission that the sales-process representations were inaccurate, strongly supporting knowledge of falsity at or before contracting (also pleaded "upon information and belief" re the staged demo environment).
4. **Puffery argument:** DataCore will call these sales "opinions." *Response:* specific, quantified statements (15,000 records, sub-second latency, "zero custom configuration") are statements of existing fact, not opinion.

### C. Count III — Negligent Misrepresentation

- Same integration-clause response as Count II (no non-reliance clause).
- **Economic loss rule:** the main threat. *Response:* under the independent-injury rule, fraudulent inducement-type claims seeking rescission/out-of-pocket damages (here: the $931,000 paid) are recoverable in tort; the claim is not merely re-packaged warranty breach. Risk: Medium. This count is the most vulnerable of the four.

### D. Count IV — Georgia UDTPA (O.C.G.A. § 10-1-370 et seq.)

**DataCore's arguments:** duplicative; economic loss rule; Texas choice of law displaces Georgia statute.
**Our responses:**
- The Georgia statute reaches discrete deceptive acts in connection with consumer/commercial transactions; the alleged misstatements (specific false capability representations) are actionable conduct independent of the breach claim.
- Choice of law: § 14.3 governs disputes "arising under this Agreement" / "this Agreement" — argue the clause does not reach statutory and tort claims predating the contract; Georgia has the dominant interest as forum and domicile of the deceived party (lex loci delicti: misrepresentations received and acted on in Georgia). Risk: Medium — the choice-of-law issue affects Counts III–IV most.
- Note the UDTPA's remedies (injunction) vs. damages structure; be prepared for DataCore's argument that no injunctive relief is available — consider whether Count IV should emphasize statutory deception remedies or be deprioritized as a bargaining chip.

### E. Cross-cutting: choice of law (§ 14.3)

- For Counts II–III, argue fraudulent inducement claims are governed by the law of the place of deception/reliance (Georgia) or, alternatively, that Texas and Georgia substantive law on fraud/inducement/economic loss is not materially different — mooting the issue at the pleading stage.
- Note Georgia's four-year fraud limitations period (O.C.G.A. § 9-3-31) applies if Georgia law governs, defusing the § 9.4 argument for the tort counts entirely.

## VII. THE WHITFORD DECLARATION — TACTICAL POINTS

1. **Attorney declaration, not a fact witness.** ¶¶ 4–7, 12–14 recite corporate facts "based on information provided" — classic inadmissible hearsay/lack of personal knowledge. Object to consideration of ¶¶ 4–7, 12–14 to the extent they support 12(b)(2)/(3).
2. **Damning admissions:** ¶ 12 concedes the "standard terms" (forum, choice of law, cap, waiver, one-year limitations) were "presented in DataCore's initial proposal and were **not modified during the negotiation process**" — this *undercuts* DataCore's repeated assertion that the clause was "negotiated" between sophisticated parties and supports our argument against treating the clause as heavily bargained-for under *Bremen/Atlantic Marine*.
3. **Omissions:** no denial of the Atlanta office, the ~14 Georgia employees, or Sousa's Atlanta base. On a 12(b)(2) prima facie review, Complaint allegations stand effectively unrebutted.
4. ¶ 14 claims implementation was managed remotely from Austin — conflicts with Complaint's allegations of Georgia-directed servicing; a fact dispute, again improper for Rule 12.
