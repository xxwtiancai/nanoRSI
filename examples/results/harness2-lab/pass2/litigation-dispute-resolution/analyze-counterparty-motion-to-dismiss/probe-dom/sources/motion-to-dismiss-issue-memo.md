## I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

DataCore has moved to dismiss all four counts of Pinnacle's Complaint on four independent grounds: (1) lack of subject matter jurisdiction; (2) lack of personal jurisdiction; (3) improper venue under the MSA forum selection clause; and (4) failure to state a claim. Based on my review of the Motion, the Complaint, the MSA, the Whitford Declaration, and the two key email communications (the Sousa–Nance pre-contractual emails and the January 12, 2023 Subramanian email), each ground is vulnerable:

- **12(b)(1):** The amount in controversy facially exceeds $75,000 ($931,000 in payments made alone); the legal-certainty standard is not met.
- **12(b)(2):** The Complaint pleads specific jurisdiction based on DataCore's Atlanta office and Sousa's Georgia-based conduct; DataCore's evidence (Whitford Decl.) conspicuously **does not deny** the Atlanta office or Sousa's location.
- **12(b)(3):** The forum selection clause is a strong argument for DataCore, but Pinnacle has substantive responses (public-interest factors, *Atlantic Marine* analysis — note DataCore's motion incorrectly relies on *Bremen/Stewart* rather than the controlling *Atlantic Marine* framework; alternatively, the fraud claims arguably fall outside a clause limited to disputes "arising out of or relating to this Agreement" if induced by fraud). Venue itself is proper under § 1391(b)(2) independent of the clause.
- **12(b)(6):** The one-year contractual limitations period (§ 9.4) is DataCore's strongest merits argument, but the January 12, 2023 Subramanian email supports both tolling/estoppel arguments and re-accrual, and factual disputes preclude dismissal. The fraud and negligent misrepresentation claims are pleaded with particularity (who, what, when, where, why) and are not barred by the integration clause, which does not contain a non-reliance/anti-reliance provision. The damages caps and consequential damages waiver raise fact questions and, in any event, limit remedies rather than defeat claims.

**Bottom line:** Recommend opposing the motion in full. The weakest points for Pinnacle are the forum selection clause and the § 9.4 limitations period; both can be contested but require development (jurisdictional discovery on the forum clause arguments; evidentiary record on discovery/limitations dates).

## III. RULE 12(b)(1) — SUBJECT MATTER JURISDICTION

**DataCore's argument:** Pinnacle's damages figures (~$4.47M total) are "speculative and inflated," particularly the $1.2M consequential damages figure attributed to Greenleaf Accounting Group.

**Pinnacle's counterarguments:**

1. **Facial sufficiency.** The Complaint alleges specific, quantified damages far above $75,000: $931,000 actually paid (21 monthly payments of $36,000 plus $175,000 implementation fee), a $2,335,000 total contract commitment, and $1,200,000 in consequential damages. Dismissal for lack of amount in controversy requires that it appear **to a legal certainty** that the claim is worth less than the jurisdictional minimum. Speculation about whether Pinnacle can *prove* damages goes to the merits, not jurisdiction.
2. **Reliance damages alone suffice.** Even excluding every contested category, the $931,000 in payments made and the $85,000 in undisclosed custom API development independently exceed $75,000 by an order of magnitude.
3. **Improper use of 12(b)(1).** DataCore offers no evidence contradicting the damages allegations — only argument. A factual 12(b)(1) challenge requires evidentiary support; DataCore has none.

**Assessment:** Strong for Pinnacle. Expect this section to be abandoned or easily defeated.

## IV. RULE 12(b)(2) — PERSONAL JURISDICTION

**DataCore's argument (per the Motion and Whitford Declaration):** DataCore is "at home" in Delaware/Texas (*Daimler*); operations, engineering, leadership, and support are in Austin; hosting servers are in Virginia and Oregon; nothing is in Georgia.

**Pinnacle's counterarguments:**

1. **Specific jurisdiction — the Complaint's allegations.** The Complaint alleges (¶¶ 12, 16): a permanent Atlanta regional office at 100 Techwood Drive NW staffed by ~14 employees; Victor Sousa, VP of Sales–Southeast, based in and operating from that office; the alleged misrepresentations made **from Georgia to a Georgia plaintiff**; Sousa signing the MSA from the Atlanta office; and implementation/services delivered to Pinnacle in Georgia. Under Georgia's long-arm statute (O.C.G.A. § 9-10-91) and *Burger King v. Rudzewicz*, claims arising out of these contacts support specific jurisdiction.
2. **The Whitford Declaration's critical omission.** Whitford testifies at length about Austin, but **nowhere denies** the existence of the Atlanta office, Sousa's Georgia location, or the 14 Atlanta-based employees. On a 12(b)(2) motion, uncontroverted allegations in the Complaint are taken as true where the defendant's evidence does not contradict them. Pinnacle should affirmatively flag this silence and, at minimum, request jurisdictional discovery (deposition of Sousa, Atlanta office lease, org charts) if the Court has any doubt.
3. ***Daimler* is inapposite.** *Daimler* and *Goodyear* address **general** jurisdiction based on "at home" contacts. Pinnacle's primary theory is **specific** jurisdiction: this lawsuit arises directly out of DataCore's Georgia-directed sales conduct. General jurisdiction (¶ 17) is the weaker alternative and should be deemphasized in the opposition brief.
4. **Purposeful availment.** DataCore deliberately stationed a regional VP in Georgia to solicit Southeast clients, secured a $2.335M contract from a Georgia business, and serviced that account in Georgia. That is textbook purposeful availment of the Georgia forum.

**Assessment:** Strong for Pinnacle on specific jurisdiction, especially given the evidentiary gap in the Whitford Declaration. Recommend attaching or seeking evidence of the Atlanta office (lease, Georgia Secretary of State registration, Sousa's email signature block as alleged in the Complaint showing "100 Techwood Drive NW, Atlanta, GA").

## V. RULE 12(b)(3) — VENUE / FORUM SELECTION CLAUSE

**DataCore's argument:** MSA § 14.2 mandates exclusive resolution in Travis County, Texas courts; the clause deserves near-dispositive deference under *Bremen* and *Stewart Org.*

**Pinnacle's counterarguments:**

1. **Wrong legal standard.** In *Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court*, 571 U.S. 49 (2013) — conspicuously absent from DataCore's Table of Authorities — the Supreme Court held that a forum selection clause is properly raised through **Rule 12(b)(3)** only as to a **forum-selection clause pointing to another federal forum**; where the clause points to a state or foreign forum, the correct vehicle is **§ 1404(a) transfer, not dismissal**. DataCore's clause points to state *or federal* courts in Travis County; if the W.D. Tex. (Austin Division) is an available federal forum, the remedy is transfer, not 12(b)(3) dismissal. This is a significant procedural defect in the motion. Additionally, under *Atlantic Marine*, venue proper under § 1391 cannot be rendered "improper" by a forum selection clause.
2. **Venue is otherwise proper.** Section 1391(b)(2) is satisfied: the alleged misrepresentations were made from (and the injury suffered in) the Northern District of Georgia, and the MSA was negotiated and performed there (Complaint ¶ 18).
3. **Scope/validity arguments (develop with facts):**
   - **Fraud in the inducement of the contract containing the clause:** If DataCore's misrepresentations induced the MSA as a whole, the forum clause — part of the induced instrument — is open to challenge. (The Supreme Court left open in *Scherk* and *Prima Paint*-line cases whether the clause itself was separately induced; pleading and discovery can address this.)
   - **Public-interest factors:** Under *Atlantic Marine*, private-interest factors are deemed waived, but public-interest factors (local interest of Georgia in redressing fraud against a Georgia company, administrative difficulties) still count — though candidly this is an uphill battle post-*Atlantic Marine*.
4. **Honest risk assessment.** This is DataCore's strongest non-merits argument. If the Court enforces the clause, the likely outcome is **transfer to W.D. Tex.**, not dismissal — which changes strategy (Texas law would then govern; consider whether opposing transfer or consenting with a reservation is the better tactical posture).

## VI. RULE 12(b)(6) — FAILURE TO STATE A CLAIM

### A. Count I — Breach of Contract

**DataCore's arguments:** (i) one-year contractual limitations period (§ 9.4) expired — Pinnacle knew of problems by August 2022 but sued March 22, 2024; (ii) liability cap (§ 11.1, $432K); (iii) consequential damages waiver (§ 11.2).

**Pinnacle's counterarguments:**

1. **Limitations — accrual and the Subramanian email.** Section 9.4 runs from when the claimant "knew or should have known of the facts giving rise to such claim." The Complaint's timeline: ADP concerns raised August 2022; latency concerns November 2022; formal notice December 5, 2022; then the **January 12, 2023 email from DataCore CTO Anil Subramanian acknowledging "performance limitations at scale that do not meet the capabilities described during the sales process."** Until that admission, Pinnacle could reasonably have believed the problems were fixable defects rather than breaches. Accrual is a fact question ill-suited to 12(b)(6), and at the latest a January 2023 accrual plus DataCore's remedial-patch efforts (April 15, 2023 patch achieving only 7,500 of 15,000 records) supports **re-accrual or continuing-breach theories** within one year of the March 22, 2024 filing.
2. **Estoppel/waiver.** DataCore's December 2022–April 2023 remediation commitments and acknowledgment of shortcomings support an estoppel or waiver argument: DataCore induced Pinnacle to defer suit while promising a fix.
3. **Affirmative-defense posture.** A contractual limitations period is an **affirmative defense**; dismissal at the pleading stage is appropriate only if the defense appears on the face of the Complaint. The Complaint's allegations (remediation, admission, partial patch) do not establish expiration as a matter of law.
4. **Damages caps do not defeat the claim.** Sections 11.1 and 11.2 limit **remedies**, not the existence of a cause of action. A breach claim survives even if damages are capped; moreover, whether the caps apply to Pinnacle's fraud-based claims (§ 11.1 speaks of liability "under this Agreement") and whether DataCore's conduct falls within any fraudulent-conduct exception are contested questions unsuitable for 12(b)(6). Pinnacle's out-of-pocket/reliance damages ($931,000) exceed the $432,000 cap anyway, so the cap reduces but does not eliminate recovery.

### B. Count II — Fraudulent Inducement

**DataCore's arguments:** insufficient particularity/plausibility; integration clause (§ 14.7) forecloses reliance.

**Pinnacle's counterarguments:**

1. **Rule 9(b) is satisfied.** The Complaint pleads with precision: **who** (Victor Sousa, VP Sales, and Derek Baines, sales engineer); **what** (a) "fully integrates with all major payroll platforms, including Paychex, ADP, and Gusto, out of the box with zero custom configuration… not a single hour on custom API work" (Jan. 10, 2022 email, Ex. A); (b) "native integration with 40+ payroll and HRIS platforms" including ADP (brochure, Rev. 11/2021); (c) "up to 15,000 concurrent temporary worker records with sub-second latency" (Feb. 14, 2022 SOW Capabilities Document, Ex. B); (d) staged demo environments (Jan. 18 and Feb. 7, 2022) presented as the "production experience"; **when/where** (dates and Georgia locations specified); **falsity** (integration required ~$85,000 in custom API work; platform handled only ~4,000 concurrent records — a 73% shortfall — and only 7,500 post-patch); **knowledge** (Subramanian's Jan. 12, 2023 admission that limitations "do not meet the capabilities described during the sales process"); **reliance and damages** ($931,000 paid; $2.335M commitment).
2. **Integration clause does not bar fraud.** A standard integration clause is **not** a clear and specific disclaimer of reliance. Under both Texas and Georgia law, to defeat fraudulent-inducement claims at the pleading stage an integration clause must contain explicit **anti-reliance/non-reliance language** (e.g., Forest Oil/Schlumberger factors in Texas; Georgia's analogous line of cases). Section 14.7's boilerplate "supersedes all prior… representations" does not state that Pinnacle did not rely on any representations. The Motion's citation of § 14.7 as dispositive overstates the law.
3. **Plausibility.** The Subramanian email — DataCore's own CTO conceding a gap between sales representations and actual capabilities — is powerful corroborating detail making fraud not merely possible but plausible under *Iqbal/Twombly*.

### C. Count III — Negligent Misrepresentation

**DataCore's arguments:** integration clause; economic loss rule.

**Pinnacle's counterarguments:**

1. **Independent injury / fraud-carve-out.** The economic loss rule generally does not bar tort recovery for **fraudulent inducement** and negligent misrepresentation claims independently causing damage (out-of-pocket losses of the $931,000 paid and $85,000 in undisclosed development — distinct from benefit-of-the-bargain losses). The Complaint pleads reliance damages separable from contract expectancy damages.
2. **Same integration-clause response as Count II** (no non-reliance language).
3. **Practical note:** This is the most vulnerable count; if any count is dismissed, likely this one or Count IV. Consider whether the opposition should emphasize Counts II and I and treat III as parasitic-but-viable.

### D. Count IV — Georgia UDPA

**DataCore's arguments:** duplicative; economic loss rule; Texas choice of law displaces Georgia statute.

**Pinnacle's counterarguments:**

1. **Georgia choice-of-law analysis is premature.** The MSA § 14.3 clause governs disputes "arising under this Agreement." Fraud-in-the-inducement and statutory deceptive-practices claims concern **pre-contractual tortious conduct**, and Georgia choice-of-law rules (lex loci delicti — Georgia, where the misrepresentations were received and acted upon and injury occurred) may apply to tort claims notwithstanding a contract choice-of-law clause whose scope is limited to contract construction. This is a contested legal question not resolvable on the face of the pleadings.
2. **Non-duplication.** The UDPA claim rests on the same deceptive acts but provides distinct statutory relief; it is not duplicative as a matter of pleading.
3. **Risk note:** UDPA remedies (injunction) are limited where no future harm is alleged; consider whether to add or substitute a Georgia antitrust/consumer claim or rely on pendent jurisdiction framing. Flag for client discussion.

### E. Cross-Cutting: Choice of Law

DataCore asserts Texas law governs everything. Counter: (i) § 14.3's scope is limited to claims "arising under this Agreement"; (ii) Georgia choice-of-law rules apply in a Georgia forum; (iii) fraud/tort claims arising from pre-contractual conduct fall outside the clause's scope; (iv) even under Texas law, most of Pinnacle's arguments (Rule 9(b) particularity, non-reliance requirement, economic-loss fraud carve-outs) prevail.

## VII. THE WHITFORD DECLARATION — SPECIFIC VULNERABILITIES

1. **Attorney declaration on merits facts.** Paragraphs 12–13 state negotiation history and "standard terms… not modified" based on hearsay from client personnel. Object to the extent the Court treats these as evidence on a 12(b)(6) motion (matters outside the pleadings would require conversion to summary judgment).
2. **Silence on Georgia.** The declaration never denies the Atlanta office, Sousa's Atlanta base, or the ~14 Georgia employees. Exploit this in the 12(b)(2) section.
3. **Contradiction with the Complaint's record.** Whitford ¶ 12 says the MSA terms were "presented… in DataCore's initial proposal and were not modified" — in tension with the Motion's characterization of "months of arm's-length negotiations" of a "negotiated" agreement. Use to undermine the "freely negotiated by sophisticated parties" framing that DataCore uses for the forum clause, caps, and limitations period.
