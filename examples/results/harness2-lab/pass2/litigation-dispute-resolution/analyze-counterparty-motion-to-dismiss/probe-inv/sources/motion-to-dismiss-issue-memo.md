## I. Summary and Overall Assessment

DataCore seeks dismissal of all four counts on jurisdictional, venue, and merits grounds. Our strongest opposition arguments are (1) personal jurisdiction (DataCore's own declaration nowhere denies the Atlanta regional office or that Victor Sousa made the alleged misrepresentations from Georgia); (2) the *Atlantic Marine* point that a forum selection clause is not properly enforced through Rule 12(b)(3); and (3) particularized fraud allegations that survive Rule 9(b) and an integration clause that — on DataCore's own declaration's admission that its standard terms "were not modified during the negotiation process" — does not satisfy the *Forest Oil/Schlumberger* test for a disclaimer of reliance.

Our most exposed points are the one-year contractual limitations period in MSA § 9.4 (the March 22, 2024 filing post-dates even the January 12, 2023 Subramanian email by more than a year) and the Georgia UDTPA count (the statute's remedy is injunctive, and the Texas choice-of-law clause complicates it). We recommend opposing the motion in full, with a targeted fallback on Counts III and IV if needed.

## III. Rule 12(b)(1) — Subject Matter Jurisdiction

**DataCore's argument:** Pinnacle's ~$4.47M damages aggregate is "speculative and inflated," particularly the $1.2M consequential figure attributed to Greenleaf Accounting Group and any Harmon Ridge Capital lending impact.

**Opposition issues:**
- Complete diversity is undisputed: Pinnacle is a Georgia citizen (incorporated and principal place of business in Georgia, Compl. ¶¶ 7–8); DataCore is a Delaware corporation with its principal place of business in Austin, Texas (MTD Statement of Facts § II.A; Whitford Decl. ¶¶ 4–5). No citizenship overlap.
- Amount in controversy: the legal-certainty standard applies (*St. Paul Mercury*). Even setting aside the contested $1.2M consequential figure, Pinnacle pleads facially supported categories of $2,335,000 (total contract value / benefit of the bargain) and $931,000 (fees actually paid — a figure DataCore itself confirms in its own Statement of Facts). Either alone clears $75,000 by an order of magnitude.
- DataCore's attack is really a dispute over *provability* of damages, not jurisdiction. At most, we should be prepared to stipulate that the $1.2M consequential figure rests on the Greenleaf quantification and to anchor the jurisdictional showing to the $931,000 in payments DataCore concedes were made.
- **Risk: low.** This ground appears makeweight.

## IV. Rule 12(b)(2) — Personal Jurisdiction

**DataCore's framing (per MTD):** Austin-centric company; reliance on *Daimler*/*Goodyear*/*Perkins* for a general-jurisdiction attack.

**Opposition issues:**
- **Specific jurisdiction is the core of the case.** The Complaint alleges (¶¶ 12, 16, 25, 28–29, 44): a permanent DataCore regional office at 100 Techwood Drive NW, Atlanta, staffed by ~14 employees servicing Southeast clients; Sousa, VP of Sales–Southeast, based in that office; the January 10, 2022 "out of the box with zero custom configuration" email sent by Sousa from the Atlanta office (signature block allegedly confirms location); the MSA signed by Sousa on DataCore's behalf while Atlanta-based; and injury suffered in Georgia. Under *Burger King*, purposeful availment/interstate activities directed at a Georgia plaintiff support specific jurisdiction under Georgia's long-arm statute and due process. This is a paradigmatic "suit arises out of" the forum contacts case.
- **Critical evidentiary gap in DataCore's papers:** The Whitford Declaration — DataCore's *only* evidence — describes Austin headquarters operations, Texas banking, Delaware incorporation, remote implementation, and Virginia/Oregon hosting (¶¶ 4–8, 14), but **nowhere denies the existence of the Atlanta regional office, denies Sousa's Atlanta location, or denies that the sales conduct occurred in Georgia.** A declaration by outside counsel, largely on "information provided to me," is also of dubious evidentiary value on 12(b)(2). We should highlight this omission prominently.
- **General jurisdiction:** *Daimler* makes the general-jurisdiction alternative (Compl. ¶ 17) weak — a 14-person regional office will not render DataCore "essentially at home" in Georgia. Treat as a fallback only; consider not leading with it.
- The forum selection clause's consent to Travis County jurisdiction does not by itself defeat Georgia jurisdiction at the 12(b)(2) stage; it is a venue/transfer consideration.

## V. Rule 12(b)(3) — Venue / Forum Selection Clause (MSA § 14.2)

**DataCore's argument:** Mandatory, negotiated Travis County forum clause; *Bremen* deference; improper venue in N.D. Ga.

**Opposition issues:**
- **Procedural defect — *Atlantic Marine*:** Under *Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court*, 571 U.S. 49 (2013), a forum selection clause pointing to another federal forum is enforced through § 1404(a) transfer (or forum non conveniens for a state/foreign clause), **not** Rule 12(b)(3), because venue proper under § 1391 is not rendered "improper" by a forum clause. DataCore's brief (per its TOC/authorities) relies on the pre-*Atlantic Marine* *Bremen*/*Stewart Organization* framework — *Stewart* in fact supports the § 1404(a) route. This is a strong threshold response; DataCore must re-file as a transfer motion.
- **Merits of the clause (for the anticipated § 1404(a) motion):**
  - Venue is otherwise proper in N.D. Ga. under § 1391(b)(2): the alleged misrepresentations emanated from and the injury was suffered in this District (Compl. ¶ 18).
  - Under *Atlantic Marine*, private-interest factors are deemed to weigh for the selected forum, but public-interest factors can defeat transfer: Georgia's strong local interest in adjudicating alleged fraud on a Georgia company by Georgia-based personnel, and the Georgia UDTPA claim invoking Georgia statutory law that a Texas court would apply less naturally. Note candidly: the public-interest argument is an uphill climb after *Atlantic Marine*.
  - Scope/fraud arguments: Compl. ¶ 39 alleges the SOW Capabilities Document was never incorporated into the MSA; the tort claims arguably concern pre-contractual conduct. Compare the asymmetry in the drafting: § 14.2 covers disputes "arising out of or **relating to**" the Agreement (broad), while § 14.3's Texas choice of law covers only disputes "arising **under**" the Agreement (narrow) — see § VII below.
  - Whitford Decl. ¶ 12 admits the forum clause was part of DataCore's "standard terms and conditions ... presented ... in [the] initial proposal and ... not modified during the negotiation process" — useful to blunt the "heavily negotiated by sophisticated parties" theme (and relevant to *Forest Oil* factors).

## VI. Rule 12(b)(6) — Failure to State a Claim

### A. Count I — Breach of Contract

**1. Contractual limitations period (§ 9.4 — one year from knew/should-have-known).**
- DataCore: discovery no later than August 2022 (ADP concerns) or November 2022 (latency), formal notice December 5, 2022; suit filed March 22, 2024 — facially late on every date.
- **Opposition arguments:**
  - **Factual accrual dispute.** "Knew or should have known of the facts giving rise to the claim" is a fact question ill-suited to 12(b)(6); Pinnacle can argue it did not know the *falsity of the pre-contractual representations* — as distinct from experiencing performance problems — until the January 12, 2023 Subramanian admission that the platform had "performance limitations at scale that do not meet the capabilities described during the sales process" (as quoted in Compl. ¶ 4).
  - **Repair/reassurance doctrine and estoppel.** DataCore's January 2023 acknowledgment plus promised and delivered April 15, 2023 patch (MTD Statement of Facts § II.C) support tolling/estoppel: DataCore repeatedly assured Pinnacle the issues would be fixed, and Pinnacle kept paying (through December 2023) in reliance.
  - **Candid risk assessment:** Even measuring from January 12, 2023, the filing on March 22, 2024 is more than 14 months later. The tolling/estoppel theory (patch promised Jan. 2023, delivered Apr. 15, 2023, still only reaching 7,500 of 15,000 records) is the strongest path — the clock arguably could not start on a claim DataCore was actively promising to cure — but this is our most vulnerable point. Build the estoppel record in the opposition (citations to the Sousa–Nance and Subramanian emails, which we must first obtain and read).
  - Also note the limitations analysis for Counts II–III is distinct (fraud accrual, and § 9.4's applicability to tort claims not "arising under" the MSA).

**2. Liability cap (§ 11.1, $432,000) and consequential-damages waiver (§ 11.2).**
- Not a basis for dismissal at the pleading stage: they limit remedies, not the existence of a claim. A complaint stating a breach claim survives; the cap is enforced at damages/liability stages.
- As applied to fraud: Texas law (like most) refuses to enforce limitation-of-liability and consequential-damages waivers so as to insulate intentional or fraudulent conduct; Pinnacle alleges knowing falsity (Compl. ¶ 42), corroborated by the alleged Subramanian admission. Enforcing § 11.2 against a punitives demand on a fraud count also raises public-policy concerns.
- Rescission-based damages (the $931,000 reliance figure) arguably sit outside a cap keyed to "liability under this Agreement."

### B. Count II — Fraudulent Inducement

**DataCore's arguments:** generalized dissatisfaction recast as fraud; integration clause (§ 14.7) forecloses reliance; Rule 9(b) particularity.

**Opposition issues:**
- **Particularity is satisfied.** The Complaint pleads who (Sousa; Baines), what ("fully integrates ... out of the box with zero custom configuration," Jan. 10, 2022 email; "up to 15,000 concurrent ... records with sub-second latency," Feb. 14, 2022 SOW Capabilities Document; "native integration with 40+ payroll and HRIS platforms," Nov. 2021 brochure; two staged demos on Jan. 18 and Feb. 7, 2022), when, where (Atlanta office / directed to Georgia), reliance (¶¶ 30, 40, 42), and falsity (4,000-record actual performance; ~$85,000 in undisclosed custom API work). These are specific, document-anchored statements — the opposite of "generalized dissatisfaction."
- **Scienter:** The alleged January 12, 2023 admission by DataCore's own CTO — that capabilities fell short of "the capabilities described during the sales process" — supports a plausible inference of knowledge, especially combined with the allegation that the demos used a specially configured staging environment (Compl. ¶ 34).
- **Integration clause does not bar fraud-in-the-inducement.** Under Texas law (*Italian Cowboy Partners v. Great Lakes Dredge*, 341 S.W.3d 323 (Tex. 2011); *Forest Oil Corp. v. McAllen*, 508 S.W.2d 812 (Tex. 2008); *Schlumberger Tech. Corp. v. Swanson*, 959 S.W.2d 171 (Tex. 1997)), a fraudulent-inducement claim survives an integration clause absent a clear, specific disclaimer-of-reliance negotiated between sophisticated parties represented by counsel. Merely boilerplate merger language (as § 14.7 contains) is insufficient. Whitford Decl. ¶ 12's admission that these were DataCore's standard terms "not modified during the negotiation process" undercuts the arm's-length-negotiation factor. *Caution:* *Forest Oil* also cautions against fraud claims contradicting clear contract terms where a specific contractual disclaimer of reliance exists — § 14.7 as quoted does not contain express non-reliance language ("Customer has not relied on any representations..."), which is our key point.
- Damages overlap issues (benefit-of-bargain vs. out-of-pocket under Texas law, *Formosa Plastics*) should be anticipated but are not pleading-stage grounds.

### C. Count III — Negligent Misrepresentation

- Same integration-clause response, plus: Texas's economic loss rule generally bars negligent misrepresentation claims arising from a contract unless there is injury independent of the economic loss or a duty arising outside the contract. The alleged concealment during the sales process (staged demos, undisclosed $85,000 custom API requirement) supplies an independent, pre-contractual duty. **Candid risk:** this is a genuinely vulnerable count under the economic loss rule; consider whether the Georgia-based public-policy and choice-of-law arguments (below) do more work, and whether to emphasize the independent-injury theory in the opposition.

### D. Count IV — Georgia UDTPA (O.C.G.A. § 10-1-370 et seq.)

**DataCore's arguments:** duplicative of contract; economic loss rule; Texas choice of law.
- **Choice of law:** § 14.3 governs only disputes "arising under this Agreement" — narrower than § 14.2's "arising out of or relating to." A statutory claim predicated on pre-contractual deceptive acts arguably does not "arise under" the MSA; Georgia has the more significant relationship (place of conduct, injury, and domicile) under Georgia's and Texas's choice-of-law rules. Whitford ¶ 12 again helps show the clause was boilerplate.
- **Economic loss rule / duplicativeness:** Georgia UDTPA claims require a likelihood of public confusion and predicate statutory violations — **candid risk:** the Georgia UDTPA provides only injunctive relief, and the Complaint (per the MTD's description) seeks damages on this count. Expect this count to be the most likely trimmed; weigh whether to concede amendment (seeking injunctive relief) rather than defend a damages theory.

### E. Cross-Cutting: Choice of Law
- Push the "arising under" vs. "relating to" drafting asymmetry: DataCore drafted the MSA; narrow reading of § 14.3 leaves tort/statutory claims to default conflict rules, which point to Georgia (misrepresentations made and received in Georgia; injury in Georgia; Georgia plaintiff).
- Whitford Decl. ¶ 12 (standard, unmodified terms) supports both this and the *Forest Oil* argument.

## VIII. Recommended Opposition Structure

1. 12(b)(1): facial challenge fails; undisputed diversity; $931,000 conceded payments alone exceed threshold.
2. 12(b)(2): specific jurisdiction from Georgia-based sales conduct; DataCore's evidence is silent on the decisive facts; declaration lacks personal knowledge.
3. 12(b)(3): *Atlantic Marine* — wrong procedural vehicle; venue proper under § 1391(b)(2); public-interest factors against transfer.
4. 12(b)(6): limitations accrual and estoppel are fact questions; caps/waivers are remedy issues, unenforceable against fraud; fraud pleaded with 9(b) particularity and supported by the CTO's admission; § 14.7 lacks the disclaimer-of-reliance language Texas law requires.
5. Fallback: if the Court is inclined to trim, prioritize preserving Counts I and II; be open to amending Count IV (injunctive relief) and to restating Count III on an independent-duty theory.
