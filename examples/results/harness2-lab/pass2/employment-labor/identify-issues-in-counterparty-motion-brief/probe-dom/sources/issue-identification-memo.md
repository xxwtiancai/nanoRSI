# ISSUE-IDENTIFICATION MEMORANDUM: OPPOSITION TO PLAINTIFF'S MOTION FOR PARTIAL SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON WARN ACT CLAIM (COUNT I)

**To:** Defense Team — Ashford & Calloway LLP
**From:** WARN Act Opposition Working Group
**Date:** June 2025
**Re:** *Delaney v. Greenleaf Organics, Inc.*, No. 3:25-cv-00491-CMW (M.D. Tenn.) — Weaknesses in Plaintiff's Motion and Recommended Opposition Strategies

---

## I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Plaintiff moves for partial summary judgment on the federal WARN Act claim (Count I), relying on (1) facility-by-facility coverage, (2) an alternative single-site aggregation theory, (3) the undisputed fact that zero days' notice was provided, and (4) arguments that neither the unforeseeable business circumstances (UBC) exception nor the faltering company exception applies. Several core facts are effectively admitted (Answer ¶ 23 admits no 60-day notice was given; headcounts and termination counts are admitted). Liability therefore cannot realistically be defeated outright at this stage; the realistic objective of the opposition is to **defeat summary judgment by creating genuine disputes of material fact on the exceptions and on the single-site/aggregation theory, and to limit damages**. This memo identifies each weakness in Plaintiff's motion and recommends the corresponding opposition strategy.

---

## II. KEY UNDISPUTED FACTS WE CANNOT CONTEST (AND SHOULD NOT TRY)

- Greenleaf is a covered employer (644 full-time employees; Answer ¶ 9).
- Nashville: 138 of 312 terminated (44.2%); Chattanooga: 72 of 189 (38.1%) — both independently exceed the 50-employee/33% mass layoff thresholds at a single site. These numbers are admitted (Answer ¶¶ 19–22) and corroborated by Greenleaf's own headcount data.
- Zero days' WARN notice was provided to employees, Tennessee, Alabama, or any local government unit (Answer ¶ 23; Voss Dep. 34, 39–40; Kowalski Dep. 52).
- CEO Voss "suspected as early as September 2024" that SunHarvest might not renew (Voss Dep. 15), and 87–94 days elapsed between the October 22, 2024 non-renewal letter and the terminations.

**Strategic consequence:** Do not oppose the motion on coverage at Nashville/Chattanooga or on the fact of non-notice. Concentrate the opposition on (a) Huntsville coverage, (b) the exceptions, and (c) damages.

---

## III. IDENTIFIED WEAKNESSES IN PLAINTIFF'S MOTION AND DEFENSE RESPONSES

### A. Huntsville Does Not Independently Trigger WARN Coverage

**Weakness in the motion:** Plaintiff's own data show only 37 of 143 employees (25.9%) were terminated at Huntsville — below both the 50-employee minimum and the 33% threshold. Plaintiff's Plant Summary table asserts "Meets 50+ Terminated Threshold: Yes (37 ≥ 50)" — a facially erroneous statement, since 37 is less than 50. Plaintiff therefore cannot obtain summary judgment on Huntsville as an independent site.

**Recommended strategy:**
- Oppose summary judgment as to the 37 Huntsville employees outright. Under 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(3), the Huntsville RIF is not a mass layoff at a single site.
- Attack Plaintiff's fallback "single site of employment" aggregation theory (see § III.B). Note the internal inconsistency: Plaintiff's motion (Section I.B) argues each facility "independently triggers" coverage while simultaneously arguing all three are one site — invite the Court to reject at least one theory.
- Preserve the Answer ¶ 31 position that the Huntsville terminations do not trigger WARN.

### B. The "Single Site of Employment" Aggregation Theory Is Legally and Factually Weak

**Weakness in the motion:** Plaintiff's alternative theory treats three plants in three different cities and two different states as one "single site of employment" based on centralized HR/payroll/management. But 20 C.F.R. § 639.3(i)(2) requires *geographic proximity*, *same purpose*, and *shared staff and equipment* for non-contiguous sites to be aggregated. Nashville, Chattanooga, and Huntsville are separate facilities over 100 miles apart in two states, with distinct workforces, no employees regularly commuting between or shared among facilities (Answer ¶ 30), and separate plant-level operations.

**Recommended strategy:**
- Argue the DOL regulations and weight of authority treat physically separate plants as separate single sites; centralized corporate services (HR, payroll, finance) are typical of any multi-plant enterprise and do not merge sites.
- Emphasize that aggregation is only Plaintiff's *alternative* theory — its weakness independently defeats summary judgment as to Huntsville, because a court granting judgment must adopt a coverage theory, and each theory has genuine factual/legal disputes.
- Develop record evidence (plant-manager declarations; distinct equipment, production lines, and supervisory structures; absence of inter-plant employee transfer) supporting site separation.

### C. Unforeseeable Business Circumstances Exception — Genuine Disputes of Fact

**Weaknesses in Plaintiff's argument:**
1. **The September "suspicion" is heavily qualified.** Voss testified SunHarvest made similar non-renewal noises in 2021 and 2022 and renewed both times (Voss Dep. 15–16); the October 22, 2024 letter was the first written communication ("That's when I knew it was real," Voss Dep. 16). A jury could find non-renewal was not reasonably foreseeable until October 22 — and even then, the *scale and necessity* of layoffs remained unforeseeable until mitigation efforts failed.
2. **Foreseeability of the layoff ≠ foreseeability of the triggering circumstance.** The UBC exception turns on whether the business circumstance (sudden loss of 31% of revenue, ~$57.97M) was reasonably foreseeable at the time notice would have been due. The proximate causes of proceeding with the full RIF on the timeline used were the January 8 Ridgewater credit denial and the January 10 FreshPath collapse — both sudden.
3. **Scope was unknown when notice would have been due.** Kowalski conceded on cross that in mid-November the scope was undetermined and numbers were not finalized until late December (Kowalski Dep. 148, 150).
4. **Premature notice would have been conditional and disruptive.** Kowalski testified the situation was "very fluid" and that if FreshPath closed, layoffs might have been avoided at one or two plants (Kowalski Dep. 150); premature notices risk self-fulfilling workforce collapse (Kowalski Dep. 152).

**Recommended strategy:**
- Frame UBC around the catastrophic, unexpected loss of the single largest customer and the failed mitigation (FreshPath LOI, Ridgewater application, Harmon Grocers and Blue Ridge Co-op outreach), culminating in the January 8–10 double blow (Chen Dep.: company "functionally unable to sustain operations beyond a single month," Chen Dep. 63).
- Argue foreseeability is a fact-bound, jury question ill-suited to summary judgment.
- **Acknowledge candidly** the adverse facts (Voss's September suspicion; the 87-day gap) and reframe: the September statements were ambiguous and historically followed by renewal; the operative unforeseeable events were the credit denial and LOI collapse in January.

### D. Faltering Company Exception — Partially Available but Requires Care

**Elements:** 29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(1) requires (i) actively seeking capital or business at the time notice would have been due, (ii) a realistic hope that the sought business/capital would permit avoiding or postponing the layoff, and (iii) a reasonable, good-faith belief that issuing 60-day notice would have precluded obtaining the capital/business.

- **Plaintiff's strongest attack:** Chen testified FreshPath's $22M would replace only ~38% of the $57.97M lost, with no reasonable prospect of replacing the remaining ~$36M (Chen Dep. 58; SUMF ¶ 21). Kowalski told Voss on December 19 that "even if FreshPath came through, we'd still need to do significant layoffs" (Kowalski Dep. 93).
- **Defensive facts:** A signed LOI existed (Dec. 5); Kowalski testified that if FreshPath closed, layoffs might have been cut to 100–120 or avoided at one or two plants (Kowalski Dep. 68, 150); Voss genuinely believed the deal would close (Voss Dep. 29). A reduction from 247 to ~100–120 terminations would have materially changed — potentially eliminated — WARN coverage at one or more sites, supporting a "realistic hope" of avoiding or postponing the mass layoff. The Ridgewater $15M line-of-credit application (Dec. 12) is pure capital-seeking and an independent ground.
- **Timing:** The exception requires active pursuit *when notice would have been due* (roughly mid-November for mid-January terminations). Post-October 22 outreach to SunHarvest (personal calls, pricing concessions, Voss Dep. 17), the November 4 emergency board meeting, the November 8 Pinnacle engagement, and November–December business development anchor this element.

**Recommended strategy:** Do not rest the opposition solely on faltering company; pair it with UBC. Assert both and argue that, at minimum, they present jury questions on foreseeability, good faith, and reasonable prospect of mitigation.

### E. "Complete Inaction" / Notice-Timing Arguments

**Weakness:** Plaintiff argues (Motion § II.C) the exceptions cannot excuse *total* failure to give notice, and that no shortened or conditional notice was given even after the Board approved the RIF on January 3 and FreshPath collapsed January 10 (Voss Dep. 34; Kowalski Dep. 64, 161).

**Recommended strategy:**
- **Damages timing:** The violation period is 60 days *minus* notice actually given. If UBC/faltering company applies, notice is excused up to the point circumstances crystallized (arguably January 10), leaving at most a 7-day exposure window for Nashville/Chattanooga and 14 days for Huntsville — a dramatic damages reduction even if liability is found.
- For the January 3–17 gap, rely on financial exigency ("weeks, not months" of cash, Kowalski Dep. 155), the company "scrambling" (Voss Dep. 34), and counsel involvement from mid-November (Kowalski raised WARN with Ashford; Answer ¶ 25 asserts action with advice of counsel).
- **Handle Kowalski's harmful testimony head-on** ("hold off on notices"; draft template never sent; her admission that mid-November notices would have achieved compliance, Kowalski Dep. 50–52, 163): argue the hold-off was a good-faith, counsel-involved judgment in a fluid situation where the scope, necessity, and even occurrence of the layoffs remained contingent on FreshPath — precisely what the exceptions address.
- **Do not argue** that notice was withheld to preserve production through the Huntsville contract week (Voss Dep. 39–40). That framing risks a bad-faith finding and civil penalties; frame the Huntsville stagger purely as operational scheduling, consistent with Answer ¶ 26.

### F. Damages — Primary Contested Ground

**Weaknesses in Plaintiff's damages case:**
1. The 60-day figure assumes zero notice credit and zero offsets; any partial application of an exception shrinks the violation period dramatically.
2. 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(1)–(2) requires offsetting interim earnings and application of individual mitigation duties; damages must be calculated employee-by-employee (Answer ¶¶ 51, 56). E.g., Briggs found comparable work in three months yet a full statutory period is claimed (Briggs Decl. ¶ 10).
3. The average daily rate of $329.47 (SUMF ¶ 31) must reflect the statutory "regular rate," not all compensation, and benefit components must be proven, not assumed.
4. The 37 Huntsville employees (~15% of the claimed class) may not be covered at all.
5. Civil penalties under § 2104(a)(3) are discretionary and should be resisted given good-faith, counsel-advised conduct.

**Recommended strategy:** Oppose any damages determination at summary judgment; pursue individualized mitigation discovery; contest the daily-rate and benefits calculations; seek exclusion of the Huntsville subclass.

### G. Procedural and Evidentiary Objections

- **Kowalski privilege issue:** Plaintiff elicited the "hold off on notices" answer over a privilege instruction (Kowalski Dep. 48–50). Move to strike or limit that testimony as revealing privileged communication content, or seek a ruling confining it.
- **Briggs Declaration:** ¶¶ 8, 11–12 contain legal conclusions ("the company violated the WARN Act"), speculation, and matters beyond personal knowledge (what "we all knew"; reports of a dozen coworkers). Move to strike those passages.
- **SUMF characterization objections:** Several SUMF paragraphs embed argumentative characterizations (e.g., ¶ 21's "no reasonable prospect"). Respond with non-admissions and record qualifications per Local Rule 56.01.
- **Citation defects:** Plaintiff's motion cites Voss Dep. 47:3–5 and 34:12–18 for propositions appearing at other pages of the excerpted transcript, and its SUMF paragraph numbering does not track the filed SUMF. Flag where material.
- **Headcount data error:** The Plant Summary's Huntsville row states "Yes (37 ≥ 50)" — facially false. Use it to attack the reliability of Plaintiff's coverage summary as to Huntsville.

## IV. RECOMMENDED OPPOSITION ARCHITECTURE

1. **Response to SUMF:** Contest or qualify every characterization; admit only the hard numbers, dates, and the fact of non-notice.
2. **Counter-statement of material facts:** Fluidity of layoff scope (Kowalski Dep. 148, 150); prior-year renewal history (Voss Dep. 15–16); first written notice October 22 (Voss Dep. 16); mitigation timeline; financial exigency (Chen Dep. 63; Kowalski Dep. 95); counsel involvement from mid-November.
3. **Brief structure:**
   - I. Summary judgment is unwarranted as to Huntsville (threshold failure; single-site theory fails as a matter of law).
   - II. Genuine disputes of material fact preclude summary judgment on the UBC exception.
   - III. Genuine disputes of material fact preclude summary judgment on the faltering company exception.
   - IV. Damages cannot be resolved on this record (offsets, mitigation, rate calculation, Huntsville exclusion).
   - V. Evidentiary objections to the Briggs Declaration and designated deposition testimony.
4. **Supporting evidence:** Declarations of Voss, Chen, Kowalski, and plant managers; Pinnacle Report; Ridgewater denial letter; FreshPath LOI and withdrawal correspondence; board minutes (Nov. 4, Nov. 8, Jan. 3).
5. **Alternative relief:** Request denial or, at minimum, limitation to liability-only as to Nashville/Chattanooga with all exception and damages questions reserved for trial.

---

## V. RISK ASSESSMENT

- **High risk:** Liability for the 210 Nashville/Chattanooga terminations is likely to be established in some form. The strongest realistic outcomes are (a) denial of summary judgment on the exceptions, (b) exclusion of Huntsville, and (c) substantial damages reduction through partial-exception timing and offsets.
- **Most harmful evidence:** Voss's September suspicion; the 87-day gap; Kowalski's "hold off" testimony and admission that mid-November notices would have achieved compliance; the two-week January 3–17 window with no notice. All must be contextualized, not ignored.
- **Avoid** any argument that notice was withheld to keep production running — that framing invites bad-faith findings and civil penalties.

## VI. CONCLUSION

Recommend filing a full opposition focused on (1) defeating coverage as to Huntsville, (2) presenting genuine disputes on both statutory exceptions, and (3) contesting the damages calculation and class-wide mechanics, together with targeted evidentiary objections and a counter-statement of facts. Partial summary judgment should be resisted even as to Nashville and Chattanooga because the UBC and faltering-company defenses present classic jury questions on foreseeability, good faith, and the reasonable prospect of saving the company.
