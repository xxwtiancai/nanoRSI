## I. Executive Summary

Plaintiff moves for partial summary judgment on the federal WARN Act claim, relying on (1) facility-by-facility coverage, (2) an alternative single-site aggregation theory, (3) the admitted fact that zero days' notice was provided, and (4) arguments that neither the unforeseeable business circumstances exception nor the faltering company exception applies. While the case presents genuine risks — most notably the admissions regarding zero notice, CEO Voss's September 2024 "suspicion," and VP-HR Kowalski's testimony that notice could have been timely sent — there are several viable grounds for opposing summary judgment in whole or in part. The strongest arguments are: (a) the Huntsville facility does not independently trigger WARN coverage (37 terminations is below the 50-employee threshold and 25.9% is below the 33% threshold); (b) the three facilities are separate single sites of employment, defeating aggregation; (c) genuine disputes of material fact exist on both statutory exceptions, particularly foreseeability; and (d) plaintiff's damages calculation is disputed and overstated.

**Recommendation:** Oppose the motion in full. At minimum, the Court should deny summary judgment as to Huntsville employees and as to both exceptions, and should decline to enter any ruling on damages.

## II. Key Factual Background (Undisputed and Disputed)

Undisputed (per Greenleaf's Answer and the deposition record):

- Greenleaf employed 644 full-time employees pre-RIF: Nashville 312, Chattanooga 189, Huntsville 143.
- SunHarvest (31% of revenue, ~$57.97M) notified Greenleaf of non-renewal on October 22, 2024, effective December 31, 2024.
- Pinnacle Consulting retained November 8, 2024; final report December 20, 2024 recommending 247 position eliminations; Board approval January 3, 2025.
- Ridgewater credit application (Dec. 12, 2024) denied January 8, 2025; FreshPath LOI (~$22M, signed Dec. 5, 2024) collapsed January 10, 2025.
- Terminations: 138 Nashville and 72 Chattanooga on January 17, 2025; 37 Huntsville on January 24, 2025.
- Zero WARN notice was provided to employees, the State of Tennessee, the State of Alabama, or any local government unit (Answer ¶ 23).
- As of January 1, 2025, cash reserves were ~$8.2M against ~$14.6M monthly operating costs (Chen Dep. 61:8–14).

Disputed or disputable:

- Whether Voss's September 2024 "suspicion" made non-renewal reasonably foreseeable (Voss Dep. 15:3–7: SunHarvest made similar comments in 2021 and 2022 and renewed both times; the October 22 letter was the first written indication).
- Whether Greenleaf reasonably believed the FreshPath LOI and Ridgewater facility could avert or materially reduce layoffs through mid-January 2025 (Voss Dep. 29:4–12; Kowalski Dep. 155:1–6 — Board approval was "contingent on FreshPath").
- Whether "severance information packets" were actually provided at termination (Kowalski Dep. 78:10–11 references packets; SUMF ¶ 32 asserts no severance was paid) — a factual discrepancy bearing on damages offsets.
- Whether the mid-November "hold off" direction reflected a considered decision to violate WARN or a reasonable, counsel-involved judgment that the scope and existence of layoffs remained genuinely uncertain (Kowalski Dep. 148:1–11, 150:1–10: scope not finalized until late December; situation "very fluid").

## III. Weaknesses in Plaintiff's Motion and Recommended Opposition Strategies

### A. Coverage / Trigger Issues

**1. Huntsville does not independently trigger WARN coverage.**
Under 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(3), a mass layoff requires at least 50 employees *and* 33% of the active workforce at a single site. Huntsville: 37 of 143 (25.9%) fails both prongs. Critically, plaintiff's own supporting exhibit contains an internal error: the Plant Summary tab of the Headcount Data marks Huntsville as "Meets 50+ Terminated Threshold: Yes (37 ≥ 50)" and "Meets 33% Workforce Threshold: Yes (25.9% ≥ 33%)" — arithmetically false on their face. Highlight this error to undermine the reliability of plaintiff's exhibit and the SUMF paragraphs (¶ 24) built on it. The motion's Section I.B concedes the point by moving immediately to aggregation for Huntsville.

**2. The single-site aggregation theory should fail.**
Plaintiff's alternative theory (Section I.C) treats all three facilities as one "single site of employment." Under 20 C.F.R. § 639.3(i)(1)–(2), separate facilities are separate single sites unless they are in reasonable geographic proximity, used for the same purpose, and share the same staff and equipment. Nashville, Chattanooga (different cities ~130 miles apart), and Huntsville (different state) are geographically separate, maintain distinct workforces and on-site supervisory structures, and no employees regularly commute between or are shared among facilities (Answer ¶ 30). Centralized HR/payroll at corporate headquarters is the norm for multi-site employers and does not collapse separate plants into a single site. Cases plaintiff cites (Teamsters Local 413; Rifkin) involved far greater operational integration. **Strategic point:** this argument cuts both ways — if the sites are separate, Huntsville is out entirely, but Nashville (138/44.2%) and Chattanooga (72/38.1%) independently trigger coverage regardless. Frame the argument accordingly: concede nothing, but recognize the single-site argument primarily protects the Huntsville subclass.

**3. Plant closing theory for Nashville is waived/unsupported.**
The motion floats a "de facto plant closing" theory for Nashville in a single sentence. All three plants remain operational (Voss Dep. 32:8–33:5). Dismiss this as unsupported and inconsistent with plaintiff's own proof.

### B. The Unforeseeable Business Circumstances Exception (29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(2)(A); 20 C.F.R. § 639.9)

**4. Foreseeability is a genuine dispute of material fact.**
Plaintiff's lynchpin is Voss's testimony that she "suspected as early as September 2024 that SunHarvest might not renew" (Voss Dep. 15:3). But her full testimony undercuts plaintiff's characterization: SunHarvest made similar "exploring alternatives" comments in 2021 and 2022 and renewed both times; the October 22 letter was the first written communication and was, in her words, "when I knew it was real" (Voss Dep. 16:23). The relevant foreseeability date is when 60-day notice would have been due (mid-November 2024 for a January 17 layoff) — and the Sudden loss of a customer constituting 31% of revenue, following years of renewal, is a paradigmatic "dramatic unexpected termination of a major customer relationship" under the DOL's own interpretive guidance (§ 639.9, example (2)). Emphasize also that even after October 22, the *existence, scope, and timing* of any layoff remained unknown and genuinely uncertain (Kowalski Dep. 148, 150: "very fluid"; scope not finalized until late December).

**5. The "87 days elapsed" argument is legally incomplete.**
Plaintiff counts days from October 22 to January 17 and concludes notice was practicable. This ignores that (a) foreseeability of *non-renewal* is distinct from foreseeability that a *statutory mass layoff at a particular site* would occur; (b) WARN requires notice only when the employer forms the requisite expectation of a covered layoff; and (c) Greenleaf was actively and reasonably pursuing alternatives (FreshPath LOI, Ridgewater application, Harmon and Blue Ridge outreach, Pinnacle's alternatives analysis) that could have avoided or substantially reduced layoffs (Voss Dep. 23:15–25:13; Kowalski Dep. 68:8–10: layoffs might have been cut to 100–120, or avoided at one or two plants).

### C. The Faltering Company Exception (29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(1); 20 C.F.R. § 639.8)

**6. The elements are genuinely disputable, not absent.**
Plaintiff argues (Section II.B) the exception fails because Greenleaf sought *customers*, not *capital*, and could not have avoided the layoffs anyway. Counters:
- The statute requires that the company be "actively seeking capital or business" that, if obtained, would have enabled it to avoid or postpone the shutdown — Greenleaf was seeking **both** business (FreshPath, Harmon, Blue Ridge) and capital (Ridgewater $15M line). § 639.8(a) expressly covers seeking "additional commitments from existing customers/clients," "business," and "capital."
- A "reasonable and realistic prospect" of obtaining the capital/business is required — the FreshPath LOI (signed, $22M) and a formal credit application under review are concrete, documented pursuits, not abstract hopes. That they ultimately failed does not negate their objective reasonableness; the test focuses on the good-faith, realistic character of the pursuit at the time.
- Timing: notice must be given "as soon as practicable." The final trigger events — Ridgewater's denial (Jan. 8) and FreshPath's collapse (Jan. 10) — occurred 7 days before the January 17 terminations; terminations followed within days, consistent with § 639.8(b)(2)(ii). Kowalski confirmed no realistic possibility of 60 days' notice after January 10 given cash reserves of $8.2M against $14.6M monthly costs (Kowalski Dep. 155:7–10; Chen Dep. 63:2–5).
- Plaintiff's argument that FreshPath would have replaced only 38% of lost revenue (SUMF ¶ 21) actually helps us: partial replacement is enough under § 639.8 to show the pursuit could have "avoided or postponed" some or all of the layoff, and Kowalski testified the scope would have shrunk materially.

**7. Plaintiff's "complete inaction" argument (Section II.C).**
Plaintiff argues that even if an exception applies, exceptions never excuse *zero* notice — notice must be given "as soon as practicable," which here would mean at least some notice in mid-November or after January 10. This is the motion's most dangerous point given the zero-notice admission. Responses:
- "As soon as practicable" is a fact-bound inquiry ill-suited to summary judgment; whether notice was practicable at any earlier point, given the fluid scope and active pursuit of alternatives, is disputed.
- On this record, the earliest defensible trigger for a firm, scoped layoff decision was January 10, 2025, and terminations followed in 7 days — arguably immediate. The January 3 Board approval was contingent on FreshPath (Kowalski Dep. 155:4–5).
- Note candidly: the 14-day gap between Board approval (Jan. 3) and the Nashville/Chattanooga terminations (Jan. 17), during which no notice was sent, is our weakest fact, compounded by Voss's admission she directed no notice preparation during that period (Voss Dep. 34:14–20). Develop affirmative evidence (e.g., operational wind-down steps, counsel involvement) to show the company was acting as soon as practicable.

### D. Evidentiary and Procedural Attacks

**8. Challenge the Kowalski excerpt on privilege grounds.**
The "hold off on notices pending the outcome of business development efforts" testimony (Kowalski Dep. 49:6–7) was extracted over a sustained privilege instruction and a motion to strike. Move to strike or limit: the direction allegedly followed a consultation with outside counsel (mid-November, Kowalski Dep. 47–49), and the substance of the communication should remain shielded. Note the tension: plaintiff cites the "hold off" instruction (SUMF ¶ 14 / ¶ 40 of Complaint) as proof of deliberate non-compliance while the privileged context is unavailable. Also note the excerpts were selected by plaintiff from a 214-page transcript; designate additional favorable passages (e.g., pp. 148, 150, 152–155) in the Rule 56 response.

**9. Attack the Briggs Declaration.**
- ¶ 12 ("The company violated the WARN Act... They had plenty of time to notify us") is an inadmissible legal conclusion — move to strike.
- ¶¶ 9–11 (hardship, three-month job search) are individualized and irrelevant to liability; relevant only, if at all, to damages and inadmissible at this stage.
- ¶ 13 (hearsay from "at least a dozen" unnamed coworkers) is inadmissible as to notice to other employees.
- Her ¶ 7 (supervisor said "everything is fine" in response to rumors) is a double-edged sword but does not establish company-wide notice practices.

**10. Dispute and narrow the SUMF.**
File a paragraph-by-paragraph response contesting, at minimum: SUMF ¶¶ 8 (Voss "suspicion" — add context), 14 (privileged; direction disputed), 21 (characterization — partial replacement still supports faltering company), 24 (Huntsville "trigger" — contradicted by statute and by the underlying numbers), and 32 (severance discrepancy with Kowalski Dep. 78:10–11). Local Rule 56.01 requires a proper response to preserve disputes.

**11. Damages (Section III of the motion).**
- Even if liability were found, partial summary judgment on damages is inappropriate: individualized interim earnings, mitigation, and benefit offsets (Answer ¶¶ 50–51) require individualized proof.
- Audit the Headcount Data: the 60-day damages figures assume a full 60-day violation period and no offsets; the daily-rate figures should be verified against payroll records. Flag the Plant Summary's arithmetical errors (Huntsville thresholds) as reason to discount the spreadsheet's reliability generally.
- The complaint alleged $4,876,200; the motion's SUMF ¶ 31 uses a $329.47 average daily rate — verify internal consistency (247 × $329.47 × 60 ≈ $4.88M; confirm).
- Reserve the civil penalty issue ($500/day to local governments) — argue no penalty is warranted given good faith (29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(3)).

**12. Temporal/definitional arguments.**
- For Huntsville: even if the sites were aggregated (they should not be), argue the January 17 (210 employees) and January 24 (37 employees) events fall outside any single 30-day aggregation window if the Court applies 90-day aggregation differently, and that no aggregation regulation compels combining separate sites. Do not overreach here, but preserve the argument.
- Consider whether any Nashville/Chattanooga terminations were "voluntary" or retirements, or employees offered transfer — audit the Termination Detail tab for offsets.

### E. Honest Risk Assessment (Weaknesses for the Client)

Candidly, the following facts materially endanger the defense and must inform settlement posture:

1. **Zero notice is admitted** (Answer ¶ 23; Voss Dep. 40:5–8; Kowalski Dep. 52:3–7) — the case will turn entirely on exceptions.
2. **Voss's September 2024 suspicion**, even with context, gives a jury a foreseeability hook; plaintiff will argue notice was due by mid-November 2024 at the latest.
3. **Kowalski's admissions**: she told Voss notices needed to go out by mid-November; she prepared a draft template November 25–26; she conceded that notice sent when she first raised the issue would have been timely ("mathematically correct," Kowalski Dep. 163:8); she personally disagreed with holding off.
4. **The two-week January 3–17 gap** with no notice activity is difficult to explain under any exception.
5. Nashville and Chattanooga independently trigger coverage on any site analysis — coverage defenses realistically benefit only Huntsville (37 of 247 employees).

## IV. Recommended Opposition Structure

1. **Response brief:** (a) Rule 56 standard — all inferences to Greenleaf; exceptions are fact questions; (b) Huntsville not a covered site; single-site theory fails under § 639.3(i); (c) genuine disputes on unforeseeable business circumstances (foreseeability, fluid scope); (d) genuine disputes on faltering company (active pursuit of business and capital; "as soon as practicable"); (e) damages cannot be resolved on this record; (f) motion to strike Briggs ¶¶ 9–13 and privileged Kowalski passages.
2. **Statement of Disputed Material Facts** contesting the SUMF paragraphs identified above, with citations to the full Voss/Kowalski transcripts, Chen testimony (financial deterioration), the Pinnacle Report (alternatives analysis), and the Ridgewater/FreshPath documents.
3. **Evidentiary motions:** motion to strike or limit per Section III.D.
4. **Affirmative evidence to develop before the response deadline:** Chen declaration on cash burn and the Ridgewater process; declaration from plant managers on facility separateness (separate workforces, supervisors, equipment); the short-term Huntsville contract documentation supporting the operational (non-manipulative) reason for the January 24 stagger; full context of the November–December board materials.
5. **Settlement consideration:** given the zero-notice admission and Kowalski's testimony, evaluate early mediation focused on a class-wide resolution with mitigation offsets, before adverse rulings on the exceptions.

---

## V. Conclusion

Plaintiff's motion is vulnerable on Huntsville coverage, on the single-site aggregation theory, on the foreseeability and faltering company fact questions, and on damages. However, as to Nashville and Chattanooga the coverage elements are effectively conceded, and the defense will rise or fall on the statutory exceptions — on which the record contains genuinely disputed facts sufficient to defeat summary judgment, though not necessarily to win at trial. Recommend full opposition on the grounds above, paired with a candid settlement assessment.
