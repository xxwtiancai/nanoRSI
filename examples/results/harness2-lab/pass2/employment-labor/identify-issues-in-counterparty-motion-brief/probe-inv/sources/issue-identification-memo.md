## I. Executive Summary

Plaintiff moves for partial summary judgment on liability under the federal WARN Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2109, based on Greenleaf's admitted failure to give any advance notice before terminating 247 employees in January 2025 (138 at Nashville and 72 at Chattanooga on January 17; 37 at Huntsville on January 24). The notice failure itself is admitted (Greenleaf Answer ¶ 23; Voss Dep. 39:13–16; Kowalski Dep. 52:3–7). The defense therefore cannot win on whether notice was given; the motion must be defeated on (1) genuine disputes of material fact regarding the "single site of employment" analysis (dispositive as to Huntsville, where only 37 of 143 employees were terminated), (2) genuine disputes regarding both statutory exceptions — unforeseeable business circumstances (§ 2102(b)(2)(A)) and faltering company (§ 2102(b)(1)) — and (3) damages exposure management. There are also significant weaknesses in our record (the Kowalski testimony and Voss's September 2024 "suspicion" admission) that counsel a fact-intensive, declaration-driven opposition rather than a purely legal one.

**Bottom line:** The motion should be winnable as to Huntsville (below the 50-employee threshold at a single site) and as to the exceptions (fact disputes for the jury on foreseeability and the company's pursuit of capital/business). Full denial is unlikely as to Nashville and Chattanooga notice liability; the realistic goals are (a) denial of summary judgment in its entirety on exception grounds, (b) exclusion of Huntsville from any liability finding, and (c) limitation of damages.

## III. Weaknesses in Plaintiff's Motion — Issue by Issue

### A. Single Site of Employment / Huntsville Below Threshold (strongest issue)

1. **Huntsville does not independently trigger WARN coverage.** A "mass layoff" requires at least 50 employees terminated at a single site and ≥33% of the workforce (29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(3)). Huntsville had 37 of 143 (25.9%) — below both the 50-employee and 33% thresholds. Our Answer ¶ 31 already denies coverage. Plaintiff concedes this by pleading Huntsville only through the alternative single-site theory (Motion § I.B–C).
2. **The three plants are in different cities and two states**, have separate workforces, separate supervisory structures, no shared/commuting employees (Answer ¶ 30). Under 20 C.F.R. § 639.3(i)(2), geographically separate facilities are presumptively separate single sites; the "reasonable geographic proximity / same purpose / shared staff and equipment" test Plaintiff quotes cuts **against** aggregation, not for it. Plaintiff's own quoted regulation undermines its single-site theory.
3. **Weakness to manage:** Evidence of centralized management, unified HR/payroll in Nashville (Kowalski Dep. 12–13; SUMF ¶¶ 28–30), a single Pinnacle plan, and a single January 3 Board approval supports aggregation. However, DOL regulations treat single headquarters' administrative functions as separate from production sites; centralized HR alone does not make separate plants a single site. Note also the Answer (¶ 26) asserts the one-week Huntsville stagger was operational (short-term contract fulfillment — Voss Dep. 38:13–17; Kowalski Dep. 80:9–12), which supports treating Huntsville separately but must be reconciled with the admission that the decision for all 247 was made January 3 (Voss Dep. 39:1–3).
4. **Strategy:** Oppose aggregation aggressively. Even if Nashville/Chattanooga liability is otherwise established, the 37 Huntsville employees (and any aggregate-damages theory premised on them) fall out. This alone defeats "partial summary judgment for the class of 247."

### B. Unforeseeable Business Circumstances Exception (§ 2102(b)(2)(A)) — genuine fact dispute

1. **Supporting facts:** The first *written* indication of non-renewal was the October 22 letter (Voss Dep. 16:16–23, 17:1–3). SunHarvest had threatened non-renewal in 2021 and 2022 and renewed both times (Voss Dep. 15:20–25) — a reasonable basis to believe the 2024 signals were likewise not final. The Answer (Preliminary Statement) alleges SunHarvest "had previously indicated that it intended to continue its longstanding relationship."
2. **Adverse facts to confront:** Voss's admission she "suspected as early as September 2024" non-renewal (Voss Dep. 45:14–16); 87/94 days elapsed from written notice to terminations (SUMF ¶ 16); Kowalski testified layoffs were "clear" as a question of "how many and where" by early November (Kowalski Dep. 30:16–19) and that notices needed to go out by mid-November (Kowalski Dep. 50:12–16); a draft notice template existed by late November (Kowalski Dep. 51:16–19). Plaintiff argues foreseeability *of the need for layoffs* — which is the correct statutory focus (foreseeability of the business circumstance, not the layoff decision). This is the motion's strongest point.
3. **Counter-strategy:** Frame the relevant inquiry as of when notice would have been due (mid-November): at that point the scope was unknown (Kowalski Dep. 148:4–11 — numbers not finalized until late December), the FreshPath LOI was live, and the RIF plan did not exist until December 20. At minimum these are jury questions on foreseeability and reasonable anticipation — sufficient to defeat summary judgment under *Anderson*.

### C. Faltering Company Exception (§ 2102(b)(1)) — genuine fact dispute but real risk

1. **Supporting facts:** Active, good-faith pursuit of capital and business to avoid layoffs: FreshPath LOI (Dec. 5), Ridgewater application (Dec. 12), Blue Ridge meeting (Dec. 18), Harmon discussions (Voss Dep. 28–30); Kowalski testified FreshPath could have cut the RIF to 100–120 positions or avoided layoffs at one or two plants (Kowalski Dep. 68:8–10; 150:4–7). Both the credit denial (Jan. 8) and FreshPath collapse (Jan. 10) post-date any date by which notice could practicably have changed the outcome; Kowalski: after January 10 there was no realistic possibility of 60 days' notice — "weeks, not months" (Kowalski Dep. 155:7–10).
2. **Adverse facts:** The exception requires that the company be actively seeking capital/business that *if obtained* would have avoided or postponed the shutdown — and Chen testified FreshPath's $22M would have replaced only ~38% of the lost $57.97M, with no reasonable prospect of replacing the remaining ~$36M (SUMF ¶ 21). Kowalski told Voss on December 19 that even with FreshPath "we'd still need to do significant layoffs" (Kowalski Dep. 93:15–17). Plaintiff also argues the exceptions never excuse *complete inaction* — no notice, no severance, no pay in lieu (Motion § II.C; Kowalski Dep. 64:8–11: "we could have at least tried to provide some notice"). The DOL position (20 C.F.R. § 639.9) is that the exceptions shorten, but do not eliminate, the notice obligation unless the employer gives notice as soon as practicable — the January 3–17 window with zero notice is our biggest vulnerability here.
3. **Counter-strategy:** Emphasize the two-week scramble post-January 3, the deteriorating cash position ($8.2M vs. $14.6M/month), and that notice given January 3 would have provided employees only 14 days — argue fact dispute on "as soon as practicable" and on whether capital-seeking was realistically capable of avoiding the layoff. Consider whether any terminal pay/benefits were provided (record says none — Kowalski Dep. 80:3–10; SUMF ¶ 32); if any payments exist, they must be surfaced for offset.

### D. The Kowalski Testimony — the single most damaging evidence

- Kowalski (VP HR, WARN-compliance owner) raised WARN compliance with outside counsel mid-November 2024, was told to "hold off on notices pending the outcome of business development efforts" (Kowalski Dep. 49:6–7), raised it again December 19, was asked to wait (51:10–13), drafted a notice template (~Nov. 25–26) but never sent it (51:16–19), and conceded on redirect that notice sent when she first raised it would have achieved compliance (163:5–8) and that she disagreed with the hold-off decision (50:8–10; 163:12–14).
- **Implications:** (a) It supports Plaintiff's "deliberate choice" narrative and undermines good-faith arguments; (b) it risks a holding that the *employer itself* treated the layoff as WARN-triggering from mid-November (an admission-adjacent fact on both coverage and foreseeability); (c) Delaney reportedly said "You knew this was coming for months" (Kowalski Dep. 83:6–8).
- **Counter-strategies:** The privileged mid-November communication with Ashford was instructed not to be answered; move to strike/redact the "hold off" testimony as reflecting privileged communications (defense counsel objected and moved to strike, Kowalski Dep. 48:9–49:10) — though the "direction to hold off" came from Voss (business decision), weakening the privilege claim. On the merits, reframe: the hold-off decision reflected genuine, documented uncertainty about whether and where layoffs would occur (Kowalski cross, 148–155), not evasion.

### E. Damages (Motion § III; SUMF ¶¶ 31–32)

1. **Average daily rate $329.47 × 247 × 60 ≈ $4.88M** (SUMF ¶ 31; Complaint's $4,876,200 figure contested in Answer ¶ 50). Attack: verify the payroll-support calculation in the Headcount Data (Payroll Summary Tab not produced in excerpts); the daily rates shown appear to be salary/365, whereas § 2104 uses the "average regular rate" over the last three years — overtime, bonuses, and benefits inclusions/exclusions need auditing per employee.
2. **Offsets:** § 2104(a)(2) credits interim earnings, and the Answer (¶ 51) asserts mitigation/unemployment offsets. Briggs's declaration (three months to find work, ¶ 10) shows offset evidence exists and is discoverable per class member — also a Rule 23 predominance argument (Answer ¶ 56).
3. **Civil penalties** (§ 2104(a)(3)) are discretionary and waived if notice is given "as soon as practicable" — tie to the exception arguments.
4. **No severance was paid** (SUMF ¶ 32) — eliminates the voluntary-payment offset under § 2104(a)(3)/(b)(3)(B) and confirms maximum exposure. Any setoff strategy therefore depends on interim earnings and mitigation.

### F. Procedural / framing points

1. **Partial SJ on liability alone is premature** where the exceptions are fact-bound jury questions; a jury must resolve foreseeability and the capital-seeking bona fides. Cite *Anderson*; moving party's burden on the exceptions (which we pleaded as affirmative defenses, Answer §§ IV) means Plaintiff must negate them — but note burden-of-proof risk: at trial we bear the burden on the exceptions, and Plaintiff argues the record compels rejection of them.
2. **Class-wide judgment inappropriate:** facility-by-facility threshold analysis (Huntsville), individualized damages, and pending class certification (no Rule 23 motion ruled on; Answer ¶¶ 55–57) all cut against class-wide partial judgment.
3. **Briggs Declaration overreach:** ¶¶ 8, 12 state legal conclusions ("violated the WARN Act," "87 days," "plenty of time") beyond personal knowledge — move to strike or object under Rule 56(c) and 28 U.S.C. § 1746. The hardship narrative (¶¶ 9–11) is immaterial to liability and duplicative of damages that are offset-eligible.
4. **SUMF citation discrepancies (flag for the opposition):**
   - The Motion cites Voss Dep. 47:3–5 and 34:12–18 for the September suspicion; the SUMF cites 45:14–16 and 28:10–18 — cite-check the transcript excerpts.
   - The Motion's Introduction asserts "nearly nine years" of Delaney's employment while the SUMF (¶ 4) says approximately eight years ten months — minor, but note.
   - Kowalski Dep. pages cited in the Motion (¶ 32, 38:7–12; 45:14–22) do not match the excerpt pagination provided (the "hold off" testimony appears at 49–50; single-plan testimony at 62–64) — the excerpts were selected by plaintiff from a 214-page transcript; demand the full transcript and any additional designations.
   - The Huntsville row of the Headcount "Plant Summary" states "Yes (37 ≥ 50)" for the 50-employee threshold — an internal error in plaintiff's own exhibit (37 < 50). Exploit this: plaintiff's own data concedes-by-error that Huntsville fails the threshold, and the "WARN Act Triggered = Yes" column is plaintiff's argument, not a fact.

## IV. Recommended Opposition Structure

1. **Response to SUMF (Local Rule 56.01):** Deny/dispute every characterizations-laden paragraph ("deliberately chose," "consciously"), admit only the hard dates and headcounts; attach defense declarations from Voss, Chen, and (if cooperative) Kowalski expanding on uncertainty, capital-seeking, and the operational reasons for the Huntsville stagger.
2. **Section I — Single site:** Each plant is a separate single site; Huntsville (37/143) triggers nothing; move to strike plaintiff's threshold column or use its internal error.
3. **Section II — Exceptions:** Both exceptions present jury questions; the critical date is mid-November, when scope was unknown, FreshPath was possible, and the Pinnacle plan did not exist.
4. **Section III — Even if liability, damages are not summary-judgment-ready:** individualized offsets, mitigation, regular-rate computation, and pending class certification.
5. **Evidentiary motions:** strike Briggs legal conclusions; move to strike/redact privileged Kowalski passages; object to plaintiff's deposition excerpts as partial designations (FRCP 32(c)(4) completeness objection — the cross-examination pages 148–155 supply essential context).
6. **Settlement posture:** With notice liability at Nashville/Chattanooga effectively conceded (Answer ¶ 23), exposure is roughly $4.9M gross less offsets; the exceptions are litigation-risk items for both sides. Recommend a damages-limited settlement discussion after the SJ ruling on Huntsville and the exceptions.
